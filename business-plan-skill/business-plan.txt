OPENING PROFILE KADAMA
How Losing Then Winning a Business Plan Competition Steered a Tutoring Startup in the Right Direction
Web: www.kadama.com • Facebook: kadama • Instagram: kadama • TikTok: @kadama

It started in a Seattle-area home. Amin Shaykho wanted to head out for the day, but his mother told him that he needed to clean the kitchen first. Because he was anxious to leave the house, he checked to see if there was an app that would connect him with someone willing to clean a kitchen. In his search, Amin discovered that the average cost for a 25-minute job was $70 to $80. The next morning, Amin began doing some research and found that the apps available to connect people needing a task done with people willing to do it focused on professional services. He found nothing available to allow people to hire others to complete a simple task for a reasonable price.

A few weeks later, Amin reconnected with a friend, Marwan El-Rukby. The two met for lunch at a gyro restaurant. They started kicking around business ideas and Amin shared his experience with the kitchen-cleaning incident. The fact that there was not an app to connect people wanting a simple task done with someone willing to do it struck a chord with Marwan. The two settled on the idea and decided to pursue it.

Kadama, the name of the app Amin and El-Rukby started working on, was a multiservice app. The idea was to connect people needing lawn care, house repairs, tutoring, and similar tasks with people willing to do them at a reasonable price. It was 2016, and Amin was just entering the University of Washington. Marwan was a year behind and the two added a third cofounder, Amin’s brother Dani Shaykho, who was still in high school. From 2016 to 2018, the cofounders developed the app. While doing so, they encountered a significant learning curve in many areas, and the three juggled Kadama with school, internships, and jobs. Finally, though, a beta version of Kadama was ready in 2018.

Marwan was a student at the University of Washington’s Foster School of Business, which has an entrepreneurship program. The cofounders entered Kadama into several business plan competitions, three of which stood out. The first competition was in early 2018. They submitted their plan but were not asked to pitch. The second competition occurred in early 2019. They had high hopes for the competition, thinking that winning would validate their idea. When the finalists were announced, they waited for their names and the name of their proposed venture to be called, but they were not. They were disappointed but, rather than becoming discouraged, they reached out to the judges and received helpful feedback. Not long after that competition, they were invited to pitch at an event that would determine which student teams would participate in the Jones & Foster Accelerator program. This program is part of the Buerk Center for Entrepreneurship at the University of Washington. It helps early-stage, student-led companies get off the ground. According to Amin, they “nailed” their presentation, resulting in them receiving an invitation to join the accelerator program.

The Jones & Foster Accelerator program ran from fall 2019 until spring 2020, when the pandemic hit. The Kadama team made a major pivot during this time. The group of mentors with whom they were working urged them to narrow the scope of their proposed service. In this sense, the advice was for them to focus on a smaller number of tasks rather than try to develop a multiservice app. In response to this counsel, they narrowed their focus initially to three tasks and then settled on one—tutoring.

Kadama is now fully up and running with the three cofounders working with the firm on a full-time basis. Here is how Kadama works. If you are a tutor, you register on the app and create a profile. Kadama prompts you to provide a detailed background that showcases your skills and experience. You then tell Kadama the subjects for which you want to serve as a tutor. If you are a student needing assistance, you access the Kadama app and create an account. You then select the subject for which you require assistance and state your budget. Kadama then shows your request to the appropriate tutors, who can opt in or opt out depending on how busy they are and your proposed budget. The tutors that opt in can message you directly to introduce themselves. Once you select a tutor, the two of you arrange a time to meet for the tutoring session.

Several aspects of Kadama’s service are unique. First, the firm offers its service in real time, if needed, and is available 24/7. The firm can do this because 30 percent of its tutors live outside the United States. So, if a student needs help at 11:00 pm for a math test the next day, a tutor in an opposite time zone is normally available to assist. Second, Kadama typically matches students with a tutor that is near their age. The company discovered that students feel more at ease and learning improves if they work with someone approximately their own age. Finally, Kadama encourages students and tutors to get to know one another beyond the tutoring relationship. This helps cultivate trusting relationships, which encourages students needing help to continue seeking out tutoring.

Along with its focus on tutoring, Kadama is known for its social media presence. Currently, the company has 375,000 followers on Instagram and 2 million on TikTok. Kadama’s social media posts rarely show the company’s service—instead, they show shortcuts and hacks for iPhones and other devices. This is a reverse-engineering approach in which the idea is to establish relationships with high school and college–age people by posting useful and engaging content. The hope is that if people start following Kadama because of its social media posts, at some point they might give the company’s tutoring service a try.

Kadama has positive momentum moving forward. Its subscriber base is increasing, and the firm is profitable. In 2022, the three cofounders—Amin Shaykho, Marwan El-Rukby, and Dani Shaykho—received a prestigious honor, as they appeared in the Forbes 30 under 30 list in the education category. In describing its list, Forbes indicates that they select 30 of “the most influential leaders and entrepreneurs who are driving change, transforming business, and making a significant impact on the world.”

A few weeks later, Amin reconnected with a friend, Marwan El-Rukby. The two met for lunch at a gyro restaurant. They started kicking around business ideas and Amin shared his experience with the kitchen-cleaning incident. The fact that there was not an app to connect people wanting a simple task done with someone willing to do it struck a chord with Marwan. The two settled on the idea and decided to pursue it.

Kadama, the name of the app Amin and El-Rukby started working on, was a multiservice app. The idea was to connect people needing lawn care, house repairs, tutoring, and similar tasks with people willing to do them at a reasonable price. It was 2016, and Amin was just entering the University of Washington. Marwan was a year behind and the two added a third cofounder, Amin’s brother Dani Shaykho, who was still in high school. From 2016 to 2018, the cofounders developed the app. While doing so, they encountered a significant learning curve in many areas, and the three juggled Kadama with school, internships, and jobs. Finally, though, a beta version of Kadama was ready in 2018.

Marwan was a student at the University of Washington’s Foster School of Business, which has an entrepreneurship program. The cofounders entered Kadama into several business plan competitions, three of which stood out. The first competition was in early 2018. They submitted their plan but were not asked to pitch. The second competition occurred in early 2019. They had high hopes for the competition, thinking that winning would validate their idea. When the finalists were announced, they waited for their names and the name of their proposed venture to be called, but they were not. They were disappointed but, rather than becoming discouraged, they reached out to the judges and received helpful feedback. Not long after that competition, they were invited to pitch at an event that would determine which student teams would participate in the Jones & Foster Accelerator program. This program is part of the Buerk Center for Entrepreneurship at the University of Washington. It helps early-stage, student-led companies get off the ground. According to Amin, they “nailed” their presentation, resulting in them receiving an invitation to join the accelerator program.

The Jones & Foster Accelerator program ran from fall 2019 until spring 2020, when the pandemic hit. The Kadama team made a major pivot during this time. The group of mentors with whom they were working urged them to narrow the scope of their proposed service. In this sense, the advice was for them to focus on a smaller number of tasks rather than try to develop a multiservice app. In response to this counsel, they narrowed their focus initially to three tasks and then settled on one—tutoring.

Kadama is now fully up and running with the three cofounders working with the firm on a full-time basis. Here is how Kadama works. If you are a tutor, you register on the app and create a profile. Kadama prompts you to provide a detailed background that showcases your skills and experience. You then tell Kadama the subjects for which you want to serve as a tutor. If you are a student needing assistance, you access the Kadama app and create an account. You then select the subject for which you require assistance and state your budget. Kadama then shows your request to the appropriate tutors, who can opt in or opt out depending on how busy they are and your proposed budget. The tutors that opt in can message you directly to introduce themselves. Once you select a tutor, the two of you arrange a time to meet for the tutoring session.

Several aspects of Kadama’s service are unique. First, the firm offers its service in real time, if needed, and is available 24/7. The firm can do this because 30 percent of its tutors live outside the United States. So, if a student needs help at 11:00 pm for a math test the next day, a tutor in an opposite time zone is normally available to assist. Second, Kadama typically matches students with a tutor that is near their age. The company discovered that students feel more at ease and learning improves if they work with someone approximately their own age. Finally, Kadama encourages students and tutors to get to know one another beyond the tutoring relationship. This helps cultivate trusting relationships, which encourages students needing help to continue seeking out tutoring.

Along with its focus on tutoring, Kadama is known for its social media presence. Currently, the company has 375,000 followers on Instagram and 2 million on TikTok. Kadama’s social media posts rarely show the company’s service—instead, they show shortcuts and hacks for iPhones and other devices. This is a reverse-engineering approach in which the idea is to establish relationships with high school and college–age people by posting useful and engaging content. The hope is that if people start following Kadama because of its social media posts, at some point they might give the company’s tutoring service a try.

Kadama has positive momentum moving forward. Its subscriber base is increasing, and the firm is profitable. In 2022, the three cofounders—Amin Shaykho, Marwan El-Rukby, and Dani Shaykho—received a prestigious honor, as they appeared in the Forbes 30 under 30 list in the education category. In describing its list, Forbes indicates that they select 30 of “the most influential leaders and entrepreneurs who are driving change, transforming business, and making a significant impact on the world.”

In this sense, the advice was for them to focus on a smaller number of tasks rather than try to develop a multiservice app. In response to this counsel, they narrowed their focus initially to three tasks and then settled on one—tutoring.

Kadama prompts you to provide a detailed background that showcases your skills and experience. You then tell Kadama the subjects for which you want to serve as a tutor. If you are a student needing assistance, you access the Kadama app and create an account. You then select the subject for which you require assistance and state your budget. Kadama then shows your request to the appropriate tutors, who can opt in or opt out depending on how busy they are and your proposed budget. The tutors that opt in can message you directly to introduce themselves. Once you select a tutor, the two of you arrange a time to meet for the tutoring session.

The firm can do this because 30 percent of its tutors live outside the United States. So, if a student needs help at 11:00 pm for a math test the next day, a tutor in an opposite time zone is normally available to assist. This is a reverse-engineering approach in which the idea is to establish relationships with high school and college–age people by posting useful and engaging content. How Losing Then Winning a Business Plan Competition Steered a Tutoring Startup in the Right Direction
Web: www.kadama.com • Facebook: kadama • Instagram: kadama • TikTok: @kadama

It started in a Seattle-area home. Amin Shaykho wanted to head out for the day, but his mother told him that he needed to clean the kitchen first. Because he was anxious to leave the house, he checked to see if there was an app that would connect him with someone willing to clean a kitchen. In his search, Amin discovered that the average cost for a 25-minute job was $70 to $80. The next morning, Amin began doing some research and found that the apps available to connect people needing a task done with people willing to do it focused on professional services. He found nothing available to allow people to hire others to complete a simple task for a reasonable price.

A few weeks later, Amin reconnected with a friend, Marwan El-Rukby. The two met for lunch at a gyro restaurant. They started kicking around business ideas and Amin shared his experience with the kitchen-cleaning incident. The fact that there was not an app to connect people wanting a simple task done with someone willing to do it struck a chord with Marwan. The two settled on the idea and decided to pursue it.

Kadama, the name of the app Amin and El-Rukby started working on, was a multiservice app. The idea was to connect people needing lawn care, house repairs, tutoring, and similar tasks with people willing to do them at a reasonable price. It was 2016, and Amin was just entering the University of Washington. Marwan was a year behind and the two added a third cofounder, Amin’s brother Dani Shaykho, who was still in high school. From 2016 to 2018, the cofounders developed the app. While doing so, they encountered a significant learning curve in many areas, and the three juggled Kadama with school, internships, and jobs. Finally, though, a beta version of Kadama was ready in 2018.

Marwan was a student at the University of Washington’s Foster School of Business, which has an entrepreneurship program. The cofounders entered Kadama into several business plan competitions, three of which stood out. The first competition was in early 2018. They submitted their plan but were not asked to pitch. The second competition occurred in early 2019. They had high hopes for the competition, thinking that winning would validate their idea. When the finalists were announced, they waited for their names and the name of their proposed venture to be called, but they were not. They were disappointed but, rather than becoming discouraged, they reached out to the judges and received helpful feedback. Not long after that competition, they were invited to pitch at an event that would determine which student teams would participate in the Jones & Foster Accelerator program. This program is part of the Buerk Center for Entrepreneurship at the University of Washington. It helps early-stage, student-led companies get off the ground. According to Amin, they “nailed” their presentation, resulting in them receiving an invitation to join the accelerator program.

The Jones & Foster Accelerator program ran from fall 2019 until spring 2020, when the pandemic hit. The Kadama team made a major pivot during this time. The group of mentors with whom they were working urged them to narrow the scope of their proposed service. In this sense, the advice was for them to focus on a smaller number of tasks rather than try to develop a multiservice app. In response to this counsel, they narrowed their focus initially to three tasks and then settled on one—tutoring.

Kadama is now fully up and running with the three cofounders working with the firm on a full-time basis. Here is how Kadama works. If you are a tutor, you register on the app and create a profile. Kadama prompts you to provide a detailed background that showcases your skills and experience. You then tell Kadama the subjects for which you want to serve as a tutor. If you are a student needing assistance, you access the Kadama app and create an account. You then select the subject for which you require assistance and state your budget. Kadama then shows your request to the appropriate tutors, who can opt in or opt out depending on how busy they are and your proposed budget. The tutors that opt in can message you directly to introduce themselves. Once you select a tutor, the two of you arrange a time to meet for the tutoring session.

Several aspects of Kadama’s service are unique. First, the firm offers its service in real time, if needed, and is available 24/7. The firm can do this because 30 percent of its tutors live outside the United States. So, if a student needs help at 11:00 pm for a math test the next day, a tutor in an opposite time zone is normally available to assist. Second, Kadama typically matches students with a tutor that is near their age. The company discovered that students feel more at ease and learning improves if they work with someone approximately their own age. Finally, Kadama encourages students and tutors to get to know one another beyond the tutoring relationship. This helps cultivate trusting relationships, which encourages students needing help to continue seeking out tutoring.

Along with its focus on tutoring, Kadama is known for its social media presence. Currently, the company has 375,000 followers on Instagram and 2 million on TikTok. Kadama’s social media posts rarely show the company’s service—instead, they show shortcuts and hacks for iPhones and other devices. This is a reverse-engineering approach in which the idea is to establish relationships with high school and college–age people by posting useful and engaging content. The hope is that if people start following Kadama because of its social media posts, at some point they might give the company’s tutoring service a try.

Kadama has positive momentum moving forward. Its subscriber base is increasing, and the firm is profitable. In 2022, the three cofounders—Amin Shaykho, Marwan El-Rukby, and Dani Shaykho—received a prestigious honor, as they appeared in the Forbes 30 under 30 list in the education category. In describing its list, Forbes indicates that they select 30 of “the most influential leaders and entrepreneurs who are driving change, transforming business, and making a significant impact on the world.”

6.1 The Business Plan
Learning Objective
6.1 Explain the purpose of a business plan.

As illustrated in the basic model of the entrepreneurial process we show in Chapter 1, writing a business plan is the last activity completed in the step of the entrepreneurial process titled “Developing Successful Business Ideas.” Writing a business plan too early is a mistake. The business plan must be substantive enough and have sufficient details about the merits of the new venture to convince the reader that it is exciting and should receive support. Entrepreneurs accumulate much of this detail during the feasibility analysis stage of investigating the merits of a potential new venture.

Despite conventional wisdom suggesting the need to do so, a relatively large percentage of entrepreneurs choose not to write business plans for their new ventures. In fact, a 2015 Wells Fargo/Gallup Small Business Index Survey (the most current information available) found that only 33 percent of business owners have a formal business plan.3 This statistic should not deter an entrepreneur from writing a business plan. Indeed, ample evidence supports the notion that writing a business plan is an extremely good investment of an entrepreneur’s time and money. The Wells Fargo/Gallup Small Business Index Survey found that businesses that have a business plan have significantly higher expectations for the years ahead than those without. It also found that those with a business plan were more likely to report that in the next 12 months they expected to increase employment, grow revenue, increase capital spending, and apply for new credit than companies without a business plan.4

6.1.1 Reasons for Writing a Business Plan
We show the two primary reasons to write a business plan in Figure 6.1. First, writing a business plan forces a firm’s founders to think systematically through each aspect of their new venture.5 This is not a trivial effort—it usually takes several days or weeks to develop an effective business plan—and the founders typically meet regularly to work on the plan during this period. An example of how much work is sometimes involved, and how a well-planned new business unfolds, involves Gwen Whiting and Lindsey Wieber, the cofounders of The Laundress, a company that sells specially formulated laundry detergents and other fabric care products. In an interview with StartupNation, the two recalled that they spent most evenings and weekends together for several months to get their business off the ground, including holidays. “We did our business plan Memorial Day weekend, our financial projections on the Fourth of July, and on Columbus Day we were looking for a manufacturer at a trade show,” Wieber recalls.6 The payoff for this level of dedication and hard work, which included preparing a formal business plan, is that Whiting and Wieber have now had a successful business for 20 years. They sell their products through their firm’s website and in many stores.

WHAT WENT WRONG?
Juicero: A Story of a Business Plan Failure

Juicero designed, manufactured, and sold the Juicero Press, which was a Wi-Fi-enabled juicer to use with fruits and vegetables. A juicer, also known as a juice extractor, is a kitchen appliance people use to extract juice from fruits and vegetables. To use a conventional juicer, you start by cutting up the fruits and/or vegetables you want to convert to juice. You feed the cut-up pieces into the top of the juicer, at which time the juicer separates the juice from the fiber. You are then left with a fresh fruit, vegetable, or combination fruit/vegetable drink that is pure juice without the pulp and fiber.

The idea behind the Juicer Press was to make the process simpler. Rather than having to buy, clean, and cut up individual fruits and vegetables, the Juicero Press worked with premade bags of chopped-up fruit and vegetable pulp that the company sold via a subscription plan for between $5 and $7 per bag. The fruits and vegetables that the pulp came from were sourced locally and the bags lasted about eight days. Juicero offered a variety of selections such as pineapple, romaine, celery, cucumber, spinach, and more. Each bag had a QR code which the Internet-connected machine scanned and verified. If a bag had reached or was beyond its expiration date, people could not use it. Once a bag was dropped into the Juicero Press, it extracted the juice from the fruit and/or vegetable pulp, producing a fresh, healthy drink in the process of doing so. Pause for a moment and go to Google Images and type “Juicero” into the search bar so you can see the Juicero Press and the premade fruit/vegetable bags.

Juicero’s sales pitch was that using a conventional juicer is too much work. By getting fruit or vegetable pulp in a premade bag, you avoid all the mess and hassle. Juicero was the juice equivalent of the popular Keurig coffee machine, which allows people to make a cup of coffee from a premade, single use package of ground coffee beans.

For Juicero, the combination of the Juicero Press machine and the fruit/vegetable packs made for an ideal combination. The company would make money on selling the Juicero Press machine and then obtain a steady stream of income from every machine sold via sales of its fruit/vegetable packs.

All of this sounds good, right? But the company failed. So, what went wrong? After raising $120 million from 2014 to 2017, the company announced that it was suspending sales of the juicers and the packets. As it turns out plenty went wrong, and Juicero’s story is a reminder that a well-developed business plan—supported by a sound feasibility analysis, business model, and industry analysis—are critical for a startup’s success. It also reminds us that a company that makes a physical product, like the Juicero Press, must make a product that people can afford, performs as advertised, and is made in a way that will not attract ridicule.

The following are three reasons the Juicer failed. Each reason represents a flaw in Juicero’s business plan.

The product was too expensive. The Juicer’s price was $699 when introduced in 2016. As a result of slow sales, the firm reduced the product’s price to $399 just a year later. Even at $399, that was a tough price point for even the most fervent believer in fresh fruit or vegetable juice to accept.

The company’s sales pitch did not make complete sense. At first glance, Juicero’s story seemed reasonable: Buy the Juicero Press machine and then buy the company’s fruit/vegetable packs to make fresh juice. But think deeper. If Juicero is already sourcing the fruits and vegetables, why not just squeeze the fruits and vegetables in its factory and then send out fresh juice? Offering this service to customers would eliminate the need for the $399 Juicero Press. When confronted with this objection, Juicero claimed that its approach produced the freshest possible juice, but it is not obvious that fruit pulp stored in a bag stays fresh longer than juice stored in a bottle.

Bad publicity. In 2017, Bloomberg published a story showing that people could squeeze Juicero’s fruit and vegetable packs by hand and, by using their hands, they could produce juice that was the same in quantity and quality from the juice produced by the Juicero Press. The story went viral and damaged Juicero’s reputation. In response, the company defended its product and process, claiming that squeezing product packs by hand made a mess and created a poor user experience. The damage, however, was done. Later, Juicero offered full refunds to any customer dissatisfied with their Juicero device.

In fairness to Juicero, the criticism that you can get the same result by squeezing the Juicero packet by hand as you can by using the Juicero Press device may be a bit unfair. You can squeeze an orange with your hands and get orange juice. But doing this creates a mess, causing most people to avoid this process. Juicero was offering a more convenient solution. What people had a hard time getting past was Juicero’s $399 price tag. Had the firm chosen to sell a less expensive machine, one that simply squeezed the juice out of the bag, it may have avoided the Bloomberg story and created a successful solution.

One must wonder what Juicero’s business plan was like. Even if a company does not have a written plan, there is an “informal” plan in the minds of the founders and the initial backers of the company. Juicero representatives certainly had a pitch deck when they pitched their investors. It is a reminder that a business plan is a thorough “walk-through” of a business venture prior to launching the venture. It is hard to imagine failing to anticipate the three fatal flaws in Juicero’s plan, described above, prior to the firm’s launch if the plan had been thorough and well researched.

Questions for Critical Thinking
For each of the three reasons that Juicero failed, describe how a well-written business plan backed up by a thorough feasibility analysis could anticipate the reason for failure.

Paul Graham, founder/manager of Y Combinator, a respected Silicon-Valley startup accelerator, says that the secret to startup success is to “Make Something That People Want.” To what degree do you think Juicero made something that people wanted?

Do you think Juicero had a written business plan? If not, what does the Juicero story teach us about the importance of having a well-researched plan?

Juicero’s failure has similarities to the failure of the Keurig Kold, an at-home soda machine, described in Chapter 3’s “What Went Wrong?” feature. For both the Juicero Press and its accompanying fruit pulp packs and the Keurig Kold, what do you think was the root cause of their failures? What is the takeaway of your conclusion for future entrepreneurs?


6.2 Who Reads the Business Plan—and What Are They Looking For?
Learning Objective
6.2 Describe who reads a business plan and what they are looking for.

There are two primary audiences for a firm’s business plan.8

6.2.1 A Firm’s Employees
A clearly written business plan, one that articulates a firm’s vision and future, is important for both the management team and the rank-and-file employees. Some experts argue that it is a waste of time to write a business plan because the marketplace changes so rapidly that any plan will become outdated quickly. Although it is true that marketplaces can and often do change rapidly, the process of writing the plan may be as valuable as the plan itself.

A clearly written business plan also helps a firm’s rank-and-file employees operate in sync and move forward in a consistent and purposeful manner. Having a business plan is particularly useful for the functional department heads of a young firm. For example, imagine that you are the newly hired vice president for management information systems for a rapidly growing startup. The availability of a formal business plan that talks about all aspects of the business and its goals and strategies can help you make sure that what you are doing is consistent with the firm’s overall direction.

6.2.2 Investors and Other External Stakeholders
External stakeholders being recruited to join a firm, such as investors, potential business partners, and key employees, are the second audience for a business plan. To appeal to this group, the business plan must be realistic and not reflective of overconfidence on the firm’s part. Overly optimistic statements or projections undermine a business plan’s credibility, so it is foolish to include them. At the same time, the plan must demonstrate clearly that the business idea is viable and offers potential investors financial returns greater than lower-risk investment alternatives. The same is true for potential business partners, customers, and key recruits. Unless the new business can show that it has impressive potential, investors have little reason to become involved with it.

Investors vary in terms of the reliance they place on formal business plans.9 Initially, many investors ask for a PowerPoint deck or the executive summary of a business plan. A PowerPoint deck is a short set of PowerPoint slides that describe a business idea, and an executive summary is a one- to two-page overview of the full plan. If investors’ interest is piqued, in some cases, they will ask for a full business plan and, in other cases, they will not do so. It is still necessary to have a business plan, however. If an investor commits, in most cases, the entrepreneur will need to have a business plan during the due diligence phase.10 Due diligence refers to the process investors go through after they tentatively commit to an investment.11 The commitment is based on a thorough investigation of the merits of the venture, whether any legal complications exist, and whether the claims made in the business plan are accurate or realistic.

A firm must validate the feasibility of its business idea and have a good understanding of its competitive environment prior to presenting its business plan to others. Sophisticated investors, potential business partners, and key recruits will base their assessment of a proposed firm’s prospects on facts, not guesswork or platitudes, as we point out in Chapter 3. The most compelling facts a company can provide in its business plan are the results of its own feasibility analysis and the articulation of a distinctive and competitive business model. A business plan rings hollow if entrepreneurs base its contents strictly on their predictions of a business’s prospects. BarkBox, which provides a monthly themed subscription box of treats, toys, and chews for dogs, is an example of a business that laid a firm foundation for its business via the feasibility analysis that the cofounders conducted early on. BarkBox is the focus of Case 3.1 in Chapter 3.

In addition to the previously mentioned attributes, a business plan should disclose all resource limitations that the business must address before it is ready to start earning revenue. For example, a firm may need to hire service people before it can honor the warranties for the products it sells. It is foolhardy for a new venture to try to downplay or hide its resource needs. One of the main reasons new ventures seek out investors is to obtain the capital needed to hire key personnel, further develop their products, lease office space, or fill some other gap in their operations. Investors understand this and, typically, experienced investors are willing to help the firms they fund plug resource or competency gaps.



6.3 Guidelines for Writing a Business Plan
Learning Objective
6.3 Discuss the guidelines to follow to write an effective business plan.

Several important guidelines should influence the writing of a business plan. It is important to remember that a firm’s business plan is typically the first aspect of a proposed venture an investor will see. If the plan is incomplete or looks sloppy, it is easy for an investor to infer that the venture itself is incomplete and sloppy.12 It is important to be sensitive to the structure, content, and style of a business plan before sending it to an investor or anyone else who may interact with or be a part of the new firm. Table 6.1 lists some of the “red flags” that surface when certain aspects of a business plan are insufficient or miss the mark.

Table 6.1 Red Flags in Business Plans
Red Flag	Explanation
Founders with none of their own money at risk

If the founders are not willing to put their own money at risk, why should anyone else?

A poorly cited plan

Entrepreneurs should build their plan on hard evidence and sound research, not guesswork or what they “think” will happen. Those writing a business plan should disclose all primary and secondary research used to complete it.

Defining the market size too broadly

Defining the market for a new venture too broadly shows that the entrepreneur still needs to identify the true target market with clarity. For example, saying that a new venture will target the music industry lacks specificity and, as a result, is not helpful. Entrepreneurs should define the market opportunity clearly and precisely. Obviously, the new venture will target a segment or a specific market within the industry.

Overly aggressive financials

Many investors skip directly to this portion of the plan. Projections that are poorly reasoned or unrealistically optimistic lose credibility. In contrast, sober, well-reasoned statements backed by sound research and judgment gain credibility quickly.

Sloppiness in any area

It is never a good idea to make a reader wade through typos, balance sheets that do not balance, or sloppiness in any area. Readers view these types of mistakes as demonstrating inattention to detail, a belief that damages the entrepreneur’s credibility.

6.3.1 Structure of the Business Plan

The business plan is often the first aspect of a proposed venture that an investor will see. Make sure it is sharp, free of typos and other errors, and visually appealing. Many investors will ask for a digital version of the business plan rather than a paper copy.

NicoElNino/Alamy Stock Photo

To make the best impression, a business plan should follow a conventional structure, such as the outline we show in the next section. Although some entrepreneurs want to demonstrate creativity in everything they do, departing from the basic structure of the conventional business plan format is usually a mistake. Typically, investors are busy people and want a plan where they can find critical information easily. When investors must hunt for something because it is in an unusual place or is not there at all, they might give up and move on to the next plan.13

Online platforms are available that employ an interactive, menu-driven approach to assist in the writing of a business plan. LivePlan (www.liveplan.com), for example, provides a user-friendly online interface for writing a business plan, along with more than 500 templates spanning various industries that allow you to tailor your plan for your industry. It even provides “live chat” opportunities with business planning professionals that can assist you. Other online platforms include enloop (www.enloop.com) and BizPlan (www.bizplan.com). While these platforms can be helpful, entrepreneurs should avoid a boilerplate plan that looks as though it came from a “canned” source. Some businesses hire consultants or outside advisers to write their business plans. Although there is nothing wrong with getting advice or making sure that a plan looks as professional as possible, a consultant or outside adviser should not be the primary author of the plan. Along with facts and figures, a business plan needs to project a sense of anticipation and excitement about the possibilities that surround a new venture—a task best accomplished by those who are creating the business.14

6.3.2 Content of the Business Plan
The business plan should give clear and concise information on all the important aspects of the proposed new venture. It must be long enough to provide sufficient information, yet short enough to maintain reader interest. For most plans, a 25- to 35-page document is sufficient. Supporting information, such as the founding entrepreneurs’ resumés, can appear in an appendix.

After completing their business plan, an entrepreneur should review it for spelling errors and grammar mistakes and to make sure that it includes all critical information. There are numerous stories about business plans sent to investors that left out important information, such as significant industry trends, how much money the company needed, or how the firm intended to use the money. One investor even told the authors of this text that he once received a business plan that did not include any contact information for the entrepreneur. This was a shame because the investor desired to learn more about the business idea.15

Style or Format of the Business Plan
Entrepreneurs should give careful thought to their plan’s appearance. It should look sharp but not give the impression that a lot of money was spent to produce it. Those who read business plans know that entrepreneurs have limited resources and expect them to act accordingly. A plastic spiral binder including a transparent cover sheet and a back sheet to support the plan is a good choice. When writing the plan, avoid getting carried away with the design elements included in word-processing programs, such as boldface or italic type, different font sizes and colors, clip art, and so forth. Overuse of these tools makes a business plan look amateurish rather than professional.16

One of the most common questions the writers of business plans ask is, “How long and detailed should it be?” The answer to this question depends on the type of business plan the entrepreneur intends to write. There are three types of business plans, each of which has a different rule of thumb regarding length and level of detail. Presented in Figure 6.2, the three types of business plans are as follows:

Summary plan. A summary business plan is 10 to 15 pages and works best for companies that are very early in their development, meaning that the entrepreneur is not ready to write a full plan. The plan’s authors may be asking for funding to conduct the analysis needed to write a full plan. Ironically, very experienced entrepreneurs also use summary business plans. They do this when they are thinking about a new venture but are not ready yet to take the time to write a full business plan. Full business plan. A full business plan, which is the most common, is typically 25 to 35 pages long. Commonly running between 40 and 100 pages in length, these plans can obviously feature a great amount of detail that provides guidance to operational managers.

If an investor asks you for a PowerPoint deck or the executive summary of your business plan rather than the complete plan, do not be alarmed—this is a common occurrence. If what the investor receives from you piques their interest, they will ask for additional information. Most investors believe the process of writing a full business plan is important, even if they do not ask for one initially. Brad Feld, a venture capitalist (VC) based in Boulder, Colorado, affirms this sentiment as the following statement demonstrates:

Writing a good business plan is hard. At one point it was an entry point for discussion with most funding sources (angels and VCs). Today, while a formal business plan is less critical to get in the door, the exercise of writing a business plan is incredibly useful. As an entrepreneur, I was involved in writing numerous business plans. It’s almost always tedious, time consuming, and difficult but resulted in me having a much better understanding of the business I was trying to create.17

A cover letter should accompany a business plan that the entrepreneur sends to an investor or other stakeholders. The cover letter should briefly introduce the entrepreneur and clearly state why the individual is receiving the business plan. As discussed in Chapter 10, if a new venture is looking for funding, a poor strategy is to obtain a list of investors and blindly send the plan to everyone on the list. Instead, each person who receives a copy of the plan should be a person the entrepreneur chose explicitly because they are a viable investor candidate.

Recognizing the Elements of the Plan May Change
A final guideline for writing a business plan is to recognize that the plan will usually change as the entrepreneur writes it and as the business evolves. New insights invariably emerge when entrepreneurs immerse themselves in writing the plan and begin receiving feedback from others. This process continues throughout the life of a company, and it behooves entrepreneurs to remain alert and open to new insights and ideas.

Because business plans usually change as entrepreneurs write them, there is an emerging school of thought that opposes the idea of writing a business plan and advocates experimentation and trial-and-error learning gleaned through customer feedback over formal planning.18 This approach, which is a part of the Lean Startup movement, espouses many excellent ideas, particularly in the area of soliciting feedback directly from prospective customers prior to settling on a business idea and business model to execute on the idea. In this text, we take the opposite position, arguing that a business plan, preceded by a feasibility analysis, represents an important starting point for a new venture and serves many useful purposes. In this sense, those developing a business plan should understand that there is no intention for it to be a static document the entrepreneur writes in isolation at their desk. Instead, entrepreneurs should anticipate that the research they conducted to complete the plan, and the preceding feasibility analysis, will place the founders in touch with potential customers, suppliers, business partners, and others and that the feedback these key people provide will cause the plan to change during the writing process.19 Entrepreneurs should also anticipate that the business itself will iterate and change after its launch, based on additional feedback. Some businesses will change more than others, based on the quality of their initial feasibility analysis and the newness and volatility of their industry. We consider these issues and related ones in the “Savvy Entrepreneurial Firm” feature.

Know When to Hold Them, Know When to Fold Them

One of the challenges business owners have is determining how closely to stick to their business plan once they launch their business. In almost all cases, some changes will need to be made because of customer feedback. The COVID-19 pandemic caused many business plans to change. Still, even in this instance, some business plans are spot-on and the worst thing a founder could do is vary from the plan. In other cases, a plan requires substantial tweaking and, in still other cases, it needs to be thrown out the window, allowing the business to start over. Next, we discuss three companies that demonstrate these different situations with their business plans.

Bloomscape—No Changes Needed
Bloomscape is an online plant company, launched in 2018, that ships plants right to your door. Its founder, Justin Marsh, comes from five generations of greenhouse growers. He grew up with his parents’ greenhouse in Detroit, learning the industry. Bloomscape’s appeal is that it delivers healthy and thriving plants to its customers. When you buy a houseplant from a big-box store or a nursery, it typically spends up to four weeks traveling from a greenhouse to a warehouse on a hot or cold truck. Following this journey, it is shipped to a store, all the while not getting the water, light, and care that it needs. Not so with Bloomscape. Its plants are cared for by Bloomscape plant experts until the firm ships them to you. The plants arrive just as you pictured them—healthy, vibrant, and sturdy. Behind the scenes, Bloomscape has learned how to keep plants at the right temperature, protect their roots, and keep them healthy as they travel from the company’s greenhouses to your door. Bloomscape’s innovative packaging holds plants securely in place, preventing damage and decreasing soil spillage. The company’s after-sale service is exemplary. Along with the customized care instructions it ships with every plant, its “Grow-How Team” is standing by to answer any questions you may have via email, chat, or tweet. While Bloomscape has enhanced its service over time, its business plan still remains largely unchanged.

Netflix—Minor Changes Needed
Netflix is a video streaming service and production studio that launched in 1997. The firm maintains a singular vision: to entertain the world. To do this, though, the firm made two changes to its business plan along the way. The first change involved how people view its content. Netflix started by renting DVDs by mail. In fact, at one point, its DVD rental service was so popular that it was the U.S. Postal Service’s largest customer. In 2007, Netflix pivoted and introduced streaming media and video on demand. Most observers believe that Netflix knew all along it would become a streaming service. The name Netflix 
 gives it away. It waited until 2007 because prior to that, streaming lacked the technological sophistication Netflix required to deliver its service as desired. The second change that Netflix made to its business plan is the decision to produce its own content. When it rented DVDs, it was renting movies and TV series made by Hollywood studios. Once streaming became available, Netflix suspected that bigger and richer media companies might start offering streaming services of their own and cut Netflix out. Because of this belief, the firm decided to become a movie and TV studio of its own. Its first hits were Lilyhammer in 2011 and House of Cards in 2013. Since that time, Netflix’s catalogue includes over 1,500 original titles, with many of these completed in countries outside the United States (e.g., Spain, Sweden, Mexico, Finland, and France, among others).

Instagram—Major Changes Needed
Instagram almost was not Instagram at all. Before Instagram became the photo-sharing hit that it is, cofounders Kevin Systrom and Mike Krieger were working on a check-in service called Burbn, which was a location-based service like Foursquare. Users could check in to locations, earn points for hanging out with their friends, and share pictures inside the app. Systrom and Krieger worked on Burbn for over a year. The cofounders believed they were ready to go, buzz was circling around the potential business, and they had $500,000 in funding. The only problem—Systrom and Krieger did not like it. To them it felt cluttered and overrun with features. They also worried about its competitiveness, at least in part because two similar services, Foursquare and Gowalla, already existed. They would have to fight it out with Foursquare and Gowalla for market share. So, they stepped back and reconsidered. They concluded that the best approach was to pick a single feature and execute on it extremely well. They stripped Burbn down to the one group or feature they liked and felt they could develop with the greatest degree of success. As it turned out, photo-sharing, comment, and like capabilities became the product feature on which Systrom and Krieger wished to concentrate. This focus became Instagram, which today is a widely popular online photo-sharing, video-sharing, and social networking service that allows its users to take pictures and videos and apply digital filters to them. Facebook acquired Instagram in 2012 for approximately $1 billion. As of spring 2023, Instagram had 2 billion active users.

6.4.1 Exploring Each Section of the Plan
Cover Page and Table of Contents
The cover page should include the company’s name, address, and phone number; the date; the contact information for the lead entrepreneur; and the company’s website address if there is one. The company’s social media accounts, if they are active, can appear on this page, too. The contact information should include an email address, a cell phone number, and a landline number if you have one. This information should be centered at the top of the page. Because the cover letter and the business plan might become separated, it is wise to include contact information in both places. The bottom of the cover page should include information alerting the reader to the confidential nature of the plan. If the company already has a distinctive trademark, the entrepreneur should place it somewhere near the center of the page. A table of contents should follow the cover letter. It should list the sections and page numbers of the business plan and the appendices.

Executive Summary
The executive summary is a short overview of the entire business plan; it provides a busy reader with everything they need to know about the new venture’s distinctive nature.20 As mentioned earlier, in many instances an investor will first ask for a copy of a firm’s PowerPoint deck or executive summary and will request a copy of the full business plan only if the PowerPoint deck or executive summary is sufficiently convincing. Thus, certainly when requested, the executive summary arguably becomes the most important section of the business plan.21 The most critical point to remember when writing an executive summary is that it is not an introduction or preface to the business plan; instead, it is meant to be a summary of the plan itself.

An executive summary should not exceed two single-spaced pages. The cleanest format for an executive summary is to provide an overview of the business plan on a section-by-section basis. Entrepreneurs should present the topics in the same order as they appear in the business plan. Also, they should prepare two identical versions of the executive summary—one that is part of the business plan and one that is a stand-alone document. The stand-alone document is available to accommodate people who ask to see the executive summary before they decide whether they want to see the full plan.

Even though the executive summary appears at the beginning of the business plan, the entrepreneur should write it last. The plan itself will evolve during the writing process, so not everything is known at the outset. In addition, if entrepreneurs write the executive summary first, they run the risk of trying to write a plan that fits the executive summary rather than thinking through each piece of the plan independently.22

Industry Analysis
The main body of the business plan begins by describing the industry in which the firm intends to compete. This description should include data and information about various characteristics of the industry, such as its size, growth rate, and sales projections. It is important to focus strictly on the business’s industry and not its industry and target market simultaneously. Before a business selects a target market, it should have a good grasp of its industry—including its most promising areas and its points of vulnerability.

Industry structure refers to how concentrated or fragmented an industry is.23 Fragmented industries are more receptive to new entrants than are industries that a handful of large firms dominate.24 You should also provide your reader with a feel for the nature of the participants in your industry. Issues such as whether the major participants in the industry are innovative or conservative and are quick or slow to react to environmental changes are the types of characteristics to convey. You want your reader to visualize how your firm will fit in or see the gap that your firm will fill. The key success factors in an industry are also important to know and convey. Most industries have 6 to 10 key factors in which all participants must establish competence as a foundation for competing successfully. Most participants try to then differentiate themselves by excelling in two or three areas. The key success factors are usually the competitive factors that are on the vertical axis of a firm’s Competitive Analysis Grid, as we explain in Chapter 5.

You also want to identify and discuss industry trends, including both environmental and business trends. The most important environmental trends are economic and social trends, technological advances, and political and regulatory changes. Business trends include issues such as whether profit margins in the industry are increasing or declining and whether input costs are going up or down. The industry analysis should conclude with a brief statement of your projections regarding the industry’s long-term prospects.

Company Description
This section begins with a general description of the company. Although at first glance this section may seem less critical than others, it is extremely important in that it demonstrates to your reader that you know how to translate an idea into a business.

The company history section should be brief but should explain the origin of the idea for the company and the driving force behind its inception. If the idea for the company is heartfelt, tell it. For example, LuminAid is a solar light company that Andrea Sreshta and Anna Stork, two Columbia University students, launched. Sreshta and Stork’s product is an inflatable plastic bag, about the size of a small pillow, with a rectangular pouch in the middle. The pouch contains a solar panel, three light-emitting diodes (LEDs), and a rechargeable battery. When inflated, the bag provides LED light; users can recharge this light in the sun. Because the solar pillow is inflatable, it packs and ships flat. That provides it with an advantage, particularly in a disaster situation. For every eight flashlights packed in a box, a total of 50 solar pillows could be in the same container. Sreshta and Stork’s motivation to design the light surfaced because of their concern for people affected by the earthquake that struck Haiti in 2010. They experienced firsthand how a disaster can have a negative effect on millions of people. One thing most victims of disasters suffer from is a lack of light. The company’s goal is to make the LuminAid light a part of the supplies commonly sent as part of disaster relief efforts.25

Sreshta and Stork’s story is heartfelt and is one with which anyone can relate. It might even cause one to pause and think, “That is a fantastic idea. That’s just the type of solution that can help people recover from a natural disaster such as an earthquake.”

A mission statement defines why a company exists and what it aspires to become.26 When written effectively and used properly, a mission statement can define the path a company takes, act as its financial and moral compass, and serve as an anchor around which decisions are made. Some businesses also include a tagline in their business plan. A tagline is a phrase that a business intends to use to reinforce its position in the marketplace. For example, Good Catch’s tagline is “Seafood Evolved.” Good Catch, which is the subject of the “You Be the VC 5.1” feature in Chapter 5, is a plant-based seafood startup. Good Catch makes plant-based fish sticks, fish fillets, fish burgers, crab cakes, and fish cakes that have the same texture and taste as regular seafood and are friendlier to oceans and the fish population.27

The products and services section should explain the firm’s product concisely and clearly. Include a description of how your product is unique and how you plan to position it in the marketplace. A product or service’s market position is how it is situated relative to its rivals.28 If you plan to open a new type of smoothie shop, for example, you should explain how your smoothie shop differs from others and how you will position it in the market in terms of the products it offers and the clientele it attracts. This section is the ideal place for entrepreneurs to start reporting the results of their feasibility analysis. If the concept test, buying intentions survey, and library, Internet, and gumshoe research produced meaningful results, this is the place to report them.

The current status section should reveal how far along your company is in its development. A good way to frame this discussion is to think in terms of milestones. A milestone is a noteworthy or significant event.29 If an entrepreneur selected and registered their company’s name, completed a feasibility analysis, developed a business model, and established a legal entity, they have reached several important milestones. The legal status and ownership section should indicate who owns the business and how the ownership is split among the parties. You should also indicate the firm’s current form of business ownership (i.e., LLC, Subchapter S Corp., etc.) if there is an agreement regarding this issue. We provide a full discussion of the different forms of business ownership in Chapter 7.

Market Analysis
The market analysis differs distinctly from the industry analysis. Whereas the industry analysis focuses on the industry in which a firm intends to compete (e.g., toy industry, fitness center industry, men’s clothing industry), the market analysis breaks the industry into segments and zeroes in on the specific segment (or target market) to which the firm will seek to appeal. As mentioned in Chapter 3, most startups focus on servicing a specific target market within an industry.

Generally, the first task entrepreneurs tackle in a market analysis is to segment the industry the business intends to enter and then identify the specific target market on which it will focus.30 This is done through market segmentation, which is the process of dividing the market into distinct segments. There is almost an endless variety of categories to use when segmenting markets such as by geography (city, state, country), demographic variables (age, income), psychographic variables (personality, lifestyle, values), and so forth. Sometimes a firm segments its market on more than one dimension to drill down to a specific segment that it thinks it can serve uniquely. For example, in its market analysis, Lively, the cell phone service with older people as the target market, probably segmented the cell phone market by age and by benefits sought. Some startups create value by finding a new way to segment an industry. For example, before Tish Ciravolo started Daisy Rock Guitar, a company that makes guitars for women, gender was not a segment in the guitar industry. Daisy Rock Guitar’s competitive advantage is that it makes guitars that accommodate a woman’s smaller hands and build.

It is important to include a section in the market analysis that deals directly with the behavior of the consumers in a firm’s target market. The more a startup knows about the consumers in its target market, the more it can gear its products to accommodate their needs. Many startups find it hard to sell products to public schools, for example, because purchase decisions are made often by committees (which draws out the decision-making process), and the funding often must go through several levels of administrators before receiving approval, if that is the decision outcome. Entrepreneurs should also include a competitor analysis, which is a detailed analysis of a firm’s competitors, in this section.31 We provided a thorough explanation of how to complete a competitor analysis in Chapter 5.

The final section of the market analysis estimates a firm’s annual sales and market share. There are four basic ways for a new firm to estimate its initial sales. If possible, entrepreneurs should use more than one method to complete this task. The most important outcome from this effort is to rely on sound judgments as the foundation for developing an estimate that is both realistic and attainable. There are two approaches that fit this category. Startups that plan to sell a product on a national basis normally use a top-down approach. This involves trying to estimate the total number of users of the product, the average price they pay, and what percentage of the market your business will garner. You may get lucky and find an article or entry that says, “XYZ firm earned gross revenues of $250,000 per year its first three years.” If the source of this data is credible and XYZ firm is comparable to your firm, you just found useful information.

The Economics of the Business
This section begins the financial analysis of a business, which receives additional attention in the financial projections. The economics of business section addresses the basic logic of how the business expects to earn profits and how many units of its product a firm must sell to “break even” and then start earning a profit.

To understand the economics of their business, an entrepreneur must identify their firm’s major revenue drivers, which are the ways it earns money. If a business sells a single product and nothing else, it has one revenue driver. If it sells a product plus a service guarantee, it has two revenue drivers, and so on. Here, too, the entrepreneur determines the size of the overall gross margin for each revenue driver. The gross margin for a revenue driver is the selling price minus the cost of goods sold or variable costs. The costs of goods sold are the materials and direct labor needed to produce the revenue driver. So, if a product sells for $100 and the cost of goods sold is $40 (labor and materials), the gross margin is $60 or 60 percent. The $60 is also called the contribution margin. This is the amount per unit of sale that remains and is available to “contribute” to covering the business’s fixed costs and producing a profit. If an entrepreneur’s business has more than one revenue driver, they should calculate the contribution margin for each. If a firm has multiple products in a revenue driver category, the entrepreneur can calculate the contribution margin for each product and take an average. (For example, if you are opening an office supply store, you may have several different computer printers under the revenue driver “printers.”) You can then calculate the weighted average contribution margin for each of the company’s revenue drivers by weighing the individual contribution margin of each revenue driver based on the percentage of sales expected to come from that revenue driver.

The next section should provide an analysis of the business’s fixed and variable costs. The variable costs (or costs of goods sold) for each revenue driver were figured previously. Add a projection of the business’s fixed costs. A firm’s variable costs vary by sales, while its fixed costs are expenses a company incurs whether it sells something or not. Next, you should discuss the company’s operating leverage. A firm’s operating leverage is an analysis of its fixed versus variable costs. Operating leverage is highest in companies that have a high proportion of fixed costs relative to their variable costs. In contrast, operating leverage is lowest in companies with a low proportion of fixed costs relative to variable costs. After completing these calculations, people preparing to work in the new venture should discuss their implications. For example, a firm with a high operating leverage takes longer to reach break-even; however, after reaching the break-even point, more of its revenues fall to the bottom line.

Entrepreneurs should also estimate their firm’s one-time startup costs and place them in a table. These costs include legal expenses, fees for business licenses and permits, website design, business logo design, and similar one-time expenses. Entrepreneurs do not include normal operating expenses in this table.

This section should conclude with a break-even analysis, which is an analysis of how many units of its product a business must sell before it breaks even and starts earning a profit. In Chapter 8, we explain the process to follow to calculate a firm’s point.

Marketing Plan
The marketing plan focuses on how the business will market and sell its product. It deals with the nuts and bolts of marketing in terms of price, promotion, distribution, and sales. For example, Allbirds, a firm producing shoes that are comfortable, stylish, and eco-friendly, may have a great product, a well-defined target market, and a good understanding of its customers and competitors, but the firm still must find customers and persuade them to buy its product.

The best way to describe a company’s marketing plan is to start by articulating its marketing strategy, positioning, and points of differentiation and then talk about how these overall aspects of the plan receive support from other variables and activities such as price, promotional mix and sales process, and distribution strategy. Obviously, it is not possible to include a full-blown marketing plan in the four to five pages permitted in a business plan for the marketing section—the goal though is to present the high points.

A firm’s marketing strategy refers to its overall approach for marketing its products and services.

Typically, a firm’s overall approach boils down to how it positions itself in its market and how it differentiates itself from competitors. GoldieBlox, for example, is a toy company that positioned itself as a business that introduces girls to the field of engineering. Women hold only about 16 percent of engineering jobs in the United States. Norway has the highest percentage of women scientists and engineers in Europe.32 Beginning with the assumption that storytelling will increase a young girl’s connection with the act of building, GoldieBlox created a set of toys for girls to use to solve problems while reading about adventures. The goal is to connect girls with the art of building and subsequently to encourage young women to pursue careers in engineering.

As we see with GoldieBlox, the marketing strategy sets the tone and provides guidance for how the company should reach its target market via its product, pricing, promotions, and distribution tactics. For example, the company will invariably promote and advertise its products in places that young women and their parents are most likely to see. Similarly, it will most likely sell its products through specialty toy stores and its own website, along with mass merchandisers such as Walmart.

The next section should deal with your company’s approach to product, price, promotion, and distribution. If you have already provided an adequate explanation of your product, you can move directly to price. Price, promotion, and distribution should sync with your positioning and points of differentiation, as described previously. Price is a particularly important issue because it determines how much money a company can make. It also sends an important message to a firm’s target market. If GoldieBlox advertised its toys as high-quality toys that are both educationally sound and environmentally friendly but also charged a low price, this would confuse people in its target market. They would think, “This doesn’t make sense. Are GoldieBlox toys high-quality products or are they not?” In addition, the lower price would fail to generate the profits that GoldieBlox needs to further develop its toys. You should also briefly discuss your plans regarding promotions and distribution in this section.

The final section should describe the company’s sales process or cycle and specific sales tactics it will employ. Surprisingly, many business plans describe a business’s overall marketing strategies, but never comment specifically about how the firm will sell its product.

Product (or Service) Design and Development Plan
If you are developing a completely new product, you need to include a section in the business plan that focuses on the status of your development efforts. Many seemingly promising startups never get off the ground because their product development efforts stall or the actual development of the product turns out to be more difficult than expected.

The first issue to address is to describe the present stage of efforts to develop your product. Most products follow a logical path of development that includes product conception, prototyping, initial production, and full production. You should describe specifically the current status of your product and provide a timeline to describe the remaining steps. If you are in the very early stages of your business and only have an idea, you should explain carefully how you will produce and test a prototype, which is the first physical depiction of a new product or service. As explained in Chapter 3, a product prototype is the first physical manifestation of a new product, often in a crude or preliminary form. The idea is to solicit feedback and then iterate. A prototype of a product, such as one of GoldieBlox’s toys, might consist of a preliminary version of the product for users to test and then report their experiences. GoldieBlox would then modify or tweak the toy based on the users’ experiences. Similarly, a prototype for a web-based company might consist of a preliminary or beta version of the site, with sufficient functionality built into the site for users to test it and then provide feedback. In some instances, a virtual prototype is sufficient. Virtual prototyping, which is becoming more popular among entrepreneurs seeking to launch their venture, uses computer-aided design, computer-automated design, and computer-aided engineering software to validate a product design before committing to making a physical prototype.33

A section labeled “Challenges and Risks” is another important part of the business plan in that entrepreneurs disclose in it any major anticipated design and development challenges and risks associated with bringing the product to market. While you want to remain upbeat, the last thing you want to do is paint an overly rosy picture of how quickly and effortlessly your design and development process will unfold. Experienced readers know that product and service development is an inherently bumpy and challenging process, and they will want insights into the challenges and risks you anticipate with your offering.

A final section should describe any patents, trademarks, copyrights, or trade secrets that you secured or plan to secure relative to the products or services you are developing. If your startup is still in the early stages and you have not acted regarding intellectual property issues yet, you should obtain legal advice so you can, at a minimum, discuss your plans in these areas. We discuss intellectual property in Chapter 12.

Operations Plan
The operations plan section of the business plan outlines how you will operate your business and how you will produce your product. You must strike a careful balance between describing this topic adequately and providing too much detail. Your readers will want an overall sense of how the business will be run, but they generally will not be looking for detailed explanations. As a result, it is best to keep this section short and crisp.

A useful way to illustrate how you intend to run your business is to first articulate your general approach to operations in terms of what is most important and what the make-or-break issues are. You can then frame the discussion in terms of “back stage,” or behind-the-scenes activities, and “front stage,” or what the customer sees and experiences. For example, if you are opening a new fitness center, the back-stage and front-stage issues might be broken down as follows:

Back Stage (Behind-the-Scenes Activities)	Front Stage (What the Members See)
Staff selection

Operations manual

Relationships with suppliers

Relationships with city government

Developing marketing materials

Employee orientation and training

Emergency plans

Member tours

Operating hours

Staff assistance

Fitness classes and programs

Fitness machines

Workshops

Monthly newsletter

Obviously, you cannot comment on each issue in the three to four pages you have for your operations plan, but you can lay out the key back-stage and front-stage activities and address the most critical ones.


This entrepreneur is writing a business plan to open a fast-casual restaurant like this one. The Operations Plan section of her plan should outline how the business will be run and the products and services that the restaurant will provide.

GaudiLab/Shutterstock

The next section of the operations plan should describe the geographic location of your business. In some instances, location is an extremely important issue and, in other instances, this is not the case. For example, one of the reasons Jeff Bezos decided to locate www.Amazon.com in Seattle is that this city is a major distribution hub for several large book publishers. By locating near these distribution facilities, www.Amazon.com gained a cost advantage that it would not have had otherwise. On a more fine-grained level, for restaurants, bars, and retail businesses, the specific location within a mall or shopping center, or a certain side of a busy street, may make a dramatic difference.

Entrepreneurs should also describe a firm’s facilities and equipment in this section. You should list your most important facilities and equipment and describe briefly how you will (or how you did) acquire them. Here you indicate if you will purchase, lease, or acquire these assets through some other means. If you will be producing a product and will contract or outsource your production, you should comment on how you will do this. If your facilities are nondescript, such as a generic workspace for computer programmers, you do not need to provide a detailed explanation.

Management Team and Company Structure
Many investors and others who read business plans look first at the executive summary and then go directly to the management team section to assess the strength of the people starting the firm. Investors read more business plans with interesting ideas and exciting markets than they can finance. As a result, it is often not the idea or market that wins funding among competing plans, but the perception that one management team is better prepared to execute its idea than the others.34

The management team of a new firm typically consists of the founder or founders and a handful of key management personnel. Here, you should include a brief profile of each management team member, starting with the firm’s founder or founders. Each profile should include the following information:

Title of the position

Duties and responsibilities of the position

Previous industry and related experience

Previous successes

Educational background

Although they should be kept brief, the profiles should illustrate each person’s qualifications and how they will allow that person to contribute meaningfully and uniquely to the firm’s success. If applicable in your situation, it is appropriate to highlight certain attributes of a management team. For example, investors and others tend to prefer team members with previous working relationships. The thinking here is that if people have worked together before and have decided to partner to start a new firm, it usually means that they get along personally and trust one another.35 You should also identify the gaps that exist in the management team and your plans and timetable for filling them. You can place the resumés of key management team personnel in an appendix to the business plan.

If your startup has a board of directors and/or a board of advisors, you should include these people as part of your management team and explain their qualifications for holding these positions. A board of directors is a panel of individuals elected by a corporation’s shareholders to oversee the management of the firm, as explained in more detail in Chapter 9. A board of advisors is a panel of experts asked by a firm’s management to provide counsel and advice on an ongoing basis. Unlike a board of directors, a board of advisors possesses no legal responsibility for the firm and gives nonbinding advice.36 Many startups ask people with specific skills or expertise to serve on their board of advisors to help plug competency gaps until the firm can afford to hire additional personnel. For example, if two web designers start a firm and do not have anyone on staff with marketing expertise, the firm might place one or two people on its board of advisors with marketing expertise to provide guidance and advice.

The final portion of this section of your business plan focuses on the intended structure of your company. Even for startups, you should outline the venture’s current structure and proposed structures to support anticipated growth. It is important that the internal structure of a company makes sense and that the lines of communication and accountability are clear. Including a description of your company’s structure also reassures the people who read the plan that you know how to translate your business idea into a functioning firm.

The most effective way to illustrate how a company will be structured and the lines of authority and accountability that will be in place is to include an organizational chart in the plan. An organizational chart is a representation of the distribution of authority and responsibility within the company. A graphical depiction of the firm’s organizational chart is the most effective form in which to present this information.

Overall Schedule
As part of the plan, an overall schedule shows the major events required to launch the business. The schedule presents milestones critical to the business’s success, such as incorporating the venture, completing prototypes, renting facilities, obtaining critical financing, starting the production of operations, obtaining the first sale, and so forth. A schedule can be extremely valuable in convincing potential investors that the management team is aware of what needs to take place to launch the venture and has a plan in place to complete those actions.

Financial Projections
The final section of a business plan presents a firm’s pro forma (or projected) financial projections. Having completed the previous sections of the plan, it is easy to see why the financial projections come last in that they express your plans in financial terms.

The first thing to include is a sources and uses of funds statement, which is a document that lays out specifically how much money a new business needs (if the intention of the business plan is to raise money), where the money will come from, and how you intend to use the money. The next item to include is an assumptions sheet, which is an explanation of the most critical assumptions on which you based the financial statements. You will base some of your assumptions on general information without citing sources to substantiate them. For example, if you believe that the U.S. economy will gain strength over the next three to five years, and that is an underlying assumption driving your sales projections, then you should state that assumption. In this instance, you would not cite a specific source in that you are reflecting a consensus view. (It is then up to your reader to agree or disagree.) You will base some other assumptions on very specific information; in these instances, you should present citations that support your assumptions. For example, if GoldieBlox has credible data showing that there is an expectation that the children’s toy industry will grow at a certain percentage each year for the foreseeable future, and this figure plays a large role in its belief that it can increase its sales every year, then it should cite the sources of its information.

The pro forma (or projected) financial statements are the heart of a plan’s financial section. Although at first glance preparing financial statements seems tedious, it is actually a straightforward process if the preceding sections of your plan are thorough. The financial statements also represent the finale of the entire plan. As a result, it is interesting to see how they turn out.

A firm’s pro forma financial statements are like the historical statements an established firm prepares; however, they are “forward looking” rather than “backward looking.” Pro forma financial statements include the pro forma income statement, the pro forma balance sheet, and the pro forma cash flow statement. Entrepreneurs usually prepare them in this order because information flows logically from one to the next. Most experts recommend three to five years of pro forma statements. If the company you are writing your plan for already exists, you should also include three years of historical financial statements. Most business plan writers interpret or make sense of a firm’s historical or pro forma financial statements through ratio analysis. People compute ratios, such as return on assets and return on sales, by taking numbers out of financial statements and forming ratios with them. Each ratio contributes uniquely to explaining the potential of a proposed business.

We present a complete explanation of how to complete pro forma financial statements and ratio analysis in Chapter 8.

Appendix
Any material that does not fit easily into the body of a business plan should appear in an appendix—resumés of the top management team, photos or diagrams of product or product prototypes, certain financial data, and market research projections. However, you want to avoid developing an appendix that adds significant length to the business plan. It should include only additional information that is vital to the plan but not appropriate for the body of the plan itself.

Putting It All Together
In evaluating and reviewing the completed business plan, the writers should put themselves in the reader’s shoes to determine if they addressed and answered the most important questions about the viability of their business venture. Table 6.4 lists the 10 most important questions a business plan should answer. These questions form a good checklist for any business plan writer to consider.

Table 6.4 The 10 Most Important Questions a Business Plan Should Answer
Is the business just an idea, or is it an opportunity with real potential?

Is the product or service viable? Does it create significant value for the customer? Is there a completed feasibility analysis? If so, what are the results?

Is the business entering an exciting, growing industry, and has the firm identified an attractive position within the industry?

Does the business have a well-defined target market?

Does the business have points of differentiation that separate it from its competitors? Are these points of differentiation sustainable?

Does the business have a sound marketing plan?

One more thought. The feasibility analysis and business plan writing stage of a startup’s journey is the ideal time to start establishing a community of true believers and early evangelists. These are people who care enough about a new business to help you by spreading the word about your interests and the actions you are taking. To varying degrees, a startup’s true believers become partners in its success.

PARTNERING FOR SUCCESS
Establishing a Community of True Believers and Early Evangelists

An important practice for entrepreneurs and their startups is to involve others in their activities, partly because that is what it takes to conduct a proper feasibility analysis and to do the research necessary to write a business plan, and partly to develop a community of people who believe in what they are doing. These people—called true believers, product champions, or early evangelists—spread awareness about a new firm’s product, not because you are paying them to do so but because they believe in what you are doing. Here are some tips for establishing a community of early evangelists. Note that the circle of early evangelists starts small and then widens out.

Tip #1: Reach Out to Family and Friends
Reach out to family and friends to involve them with what you are doing and to ask them to spread the word. The only caveat is to make sure that if you ask for feedback, be sure to let people know that you truly want candid feedback from them. Several of the entrepreneurs leading their startups we feature in this text reached out to family and friends early on. For example, to test the first iterations of their all-natural sparkling water, Aura Bora’s cofounders invited friends to their house to taste their product’s different flavors. Casper, an online mattress company, conducted beta tests of its product. In the final stages of testing the company relied on a small group of family and friends, presumably because they wanted to keep the circle small and only show their product to trustworthy people. When you ask people to help—particularly people who care about you—they will more than likely take an interest in what you are doing. Ask them if they would like to receive regular updates. They may become your first customers and your most fervent evangelists.

Tip #2: Engage Your Personal Networks
Engaging your personal network is the second source of true believers and early evangelists. We have seen examples of this in different features in this text. For instance, David Heath, one of the founders of Bombas, handed out early prototypes of Bombas socks at a gym to which he belonged. Doing this paid off in that when he ran into the same people a week or so later, they told him the socks were great and asked where they could buy them. Similarly, early on, Kaylin Marcotte, founder of JIGGY Puzzles (the focal firm in Case 13.1 in Chapter 13), scoured art fairs looking for female artists to feature their artwork on her puzzles. She was able to persuade a small group of emerging female artists to work with her, and their artwork was the first to appear on JIGGY puzzles. Similarly, several of the student startups featured in the chapters’ “Opening Profiles” relied heavily on their college networks to help get their businesses off the ground. The founders of Monarc, which makes the robotic quarterback named the Seeker, became connected with Nate Pierotti through a mutual friend. Pierotti was a student at the University of Iowa and had an in with the football team. Monarc did the early testing of its device at the Iowa indoor practice facility.

Like friends and family, when you engage your network or develop a new one, you have a chance for people to become invested in what you are doing and seeking to accomplish. Sometimes, this can result in big payoffs. In Monarc’s case, by testing its device in the Iowa indoor football practice facility, the firm received valuable feedback and advice from both players and coaches who took an interest in their device.

Tip #3: Keep in Touch with Other Early Testers
Other early testers, who test prototypes of your business idea and/or give you feedback in other ways, can also become early true believers and evangelists. Send them updates and ask them to follow you on social media. Recall Casper’s sales took off during the firm’s first month of operation. According to the case, many people were a part of Casper’s initial testing activities. These people were serious about sleep, and many became Casper evangelists, posting positive comments on social media or speaking out in other ways.

Also, identify the influencers among your family and friends, personal network, and early testers, and reach out to them individually. Keep them abreast of actions you are taking to move forward with your idea. Find additional ways for them to engage with your product and company.

Tip #4: Form a Community of Early Adopters
Your true believers and early evangelists will help form a community of early adopters. Communities typically form around interests, practices (e.g., hobbies or professions) or location. For example, remember that BarkBox’s subscribers receive a monthly BarkBox for their dogs, which includes treats and toys for them. The first several months, BarkBox’s cofounders asked friends and family to help them and those working with their firm pack their initial BarkBox orders. In turn, many of those who volunteered to help subsequently became subscribers themselves. You can also form a community of early adopters by participating in online or offline groups. Follow people on Instagram in your space. Often, they will follow you in return and, if you post interesting material, they will become familiar with you and what you are doing. Similarly, if you are active, whether it is via a crowdfunding campaign, blog postings, or some other channel, such activities can pay huge dividends. For example, Bombas received a huge boost from its Shark Tank appearance. Shark Tank became aware of Bombas via its Indiegogo crowdfunding campaign.

Questions for Critical Thinking
What are ways to get people to care about your product or service? Why is it important to establish a community of true believers and early evangelists? Wouldn’t it be easier to acquire customers via traditional advertising?

Why are the people whom entrepreneurs ask to test a prototype of a product or provide feedback in some other way ideal candidates to become true believers and earlier evangelists?

In your opinion, and using a scale of 1–10 (10 is high), how prominent a role does social media play typically in helping companies gain early adopters for their product?

Brainstorm other ways to establish a community of true believers and evangelists that we did not mention in this feature.



6.5 Presenting the Business Plan to Investors
Learning Objective
6.5 Explain how to effectively present a business plan to potential investors.

If the business plan successfully elicits the interest of a potential investor, the next step is to meet with the investor and present the plan in person. Typically, the investor will want to meet with the firm’s founders. Because investors ultimately fund only a few ventures, the founders of a new firm should make as positive an impression on the investor as possible.

The first meeting with an investor is generally very short, about one hour.37 The investor will typically ask the firm to make a 15- to 20-minute presentation using PowerPoint slides and use the rest of the time to ask questions. If the investor reacts positively to the presentation and wants to learn more about the venture, presenters receive an opportunity to meet a second time with the investor and their partners. Typically, this meeting lasts longer and requires a more thorough presentation.

6.5.1 The Oral Presentation of a Business Plan
When asked to meet with an investor, the founders of a new venture should prepare a set of PowerPoint slides that will fill the time slot allowed for the presentation portion of the meeting. The same format applies to most business plan competitions. The first rule in making an oral presentation is to follow instructions. If an investor tells an entrepreneur they have one hour and that the hour will consist of a 20-minute presentation and a 40-minute question-and-answer period, the presentation should not exceed 20 minutes. The presentation should be smooth and one for which the entrepreneur prepared carefully. The slides should be sharp and clutter free.


It’s important to practice the oral presentation of a business plan before pitching it to investors. Here, a young entrepreneur is in the midst of a practice pitch to a group of people at his university.

imtmphoto/Alamy Stock Photo

The entrepreneur should arrive at the appointment on time and ready to move forward with the presentation. If the entrepreneur requires any audiovisual equipment, they should bring it with them in case the investor does not have it. You should make these arrangements prior to the meeting. The presentation should consist of plain talk and should avoid technical jargon. Startup entrepreneurs may mistakenly spend too much time talking about the technology that will go into a new product and not enough time talking about the business itself. We display the most important issues to cover in the presentation and how to present them in Table 6.5. This presentation format features 12 slides. A mistake entrepreneurs often make is to prepare too many slides and then try to rush through them during a 20-minute presentation.

Table 6.5 Twelve PowerPoint Slides to Include in an Investor Presentation
Topic	Explanation
1. Title slide

Introduce the presentation with your company’s name, the founder’s or founders’ names, and the company logo, if available.

2. Problem

Briefly state the problem you intend to solve or the need you intend to fill.

3. Solution

Explain how your firm will solve the problem or how it will satisfy the identified need.

4. Opportunity and target market

Describe your specific target market. Talk about business and environmental trends that are providing your target market momentum.

5. Technology

This slide is optional but is normally included. Talk about your technology or any unusual aspects of your product or service; however, do not talk in an overly technical manner. Make your descriptions easy to understand and interesting for your audience.

6. Competition

Explain specifically the firm’s competitive advantage in the marketplace and how it will compete against more established competitors.

7. Marketing and sales

Describe your overall marketing strategy. Talk about your sales process. If you conducted primary research regarding how people feel about your product, report the results here.

8. Management team

Describe your existing management team. Explain how the team came together and how the members’ backgrounds and expertise are keys to your firm’s success. If you have a board of advisors or board of directors, briefly mention the key individuals involved. If you have gaps in your team, explain how and when you intend to solve this problem.

9. Financial projections

Briefly discuss the financials. Stress when the firm will achieve profitability, how much capital it will take to get there, and when its cash flow will break even. Use additional slides, if needed, to display all relevant data and information, but do not go into detail.

10. Current status

Describe the status of your firm currently in the context of the milestones you have reached. Do not diminish the value of your accomplishments.

11. Financing sought

Lay out specifically how much financing you are seeking and how you would use the money.

12. Summary

Bring the presentation to a close. Summarize the strongest points of your venture and your team. Solicit feedback from your audience.

Source: B. Barringer, Preparing Effective Business Plans: An Entrepreneurial Approach, 2nd Edition, © 2015, 247–259. Adapted by permission of Pearson Education, Inc., Upper Saddle River, NJ.

6.5.2 Questions and Feedback to Expect from Investors
Whether in the initial meeting or on subsequent occasions, an entrepreneur will receive a host of questions from potential investors. The astute and prepared entrepreneur has a solid idea of what to expect and prepares possible responses for anticipated queries in advance. Because investors often come across as being very critical,38 it is easy for an entrepreneur to become discouraged, particularly if the investor seems to be poking holes in every aspect of the business plan. The same dynamic typifies the question-and-answer sessions that follow presentations in business plan competitions. In fact, an investor who can identify weaknesses in a business plan or presentation does a favor for the entrepreneur. This is because the entrepreneur can take the investor’s feedback to heart and use it to improve the business plan and/or the presentation.

In the first meeting, investors typically focus on whether a real opportunity exists and whether the management team has the experience and skills to pull off the venture. The investor will also try to determine if the entrepreneur has a high degree of confidence in their venture. The question-and-answer period is extremely important. Here investors are typically looking for how well entrepreneurs think on their feet and how knowledgeable they are about the business venture. Michael Rovner, a partner of Rob Adam’s at AV Labs, put it this way: “We ask a lot of peripheral questions. We might not want answers—we just want to evaluate the entrepreneur’s thought process.”


Chapter Summary
LO 6.1 A business plan is a written narrative describing what a new business intends to accomplish and how it plans to achieve its goals. For most new businesses, the business plan is a dual-purpose document that is for use inside and outside the firm. Inside the firm, it helps the company develop a road map to follow in executing its strategies. Outside the firm, it acquaints potential investors and other stakeholders with the business opportunity the firm is pursuing and describes how the business will pursue that opportunity.

LO 6.2 Employees and potential investors and other external stakeholders are the two primary audiences for a firm’s business plan. There are different kinds of business plans. For example, a summary business plan is 10 to 15 pages and works best for companies in the early stages of development. These companies lack the information needed for a full business plan but can develop a summary business plan to see if potential investors are interested in their idea. A full business plan, typically 25 to 35 pages, spells out a company’s operations and plans in much more detail than a summary business plan and is the usual format for a business plan an entrepreneur prepares for an investor. Entrepreneurs usually prepare an operational business plan for an internal audience. It is 40 to 100 pages long and provides a blueprint for a company’s operations.

LO 6.3 Guidelines are available for those writing a business plan to follow. Adhering to these guidelines increases the probability that an entrepreneur will develop an effective business plan. The structure of the plan is the first guideline to consider. The advice here is that people should use a conventional structure when developing a business plan. Doing this allows business investors to focus on the parts of a plan that are critical to their decision-making process. Second, entrepreneurs should work hard to develop a business plan that is concise and clear. The plan should include all important aspects of the proposed venture. Once written, you should check for grammar errors and spelling mistakes and to verify that you included all vital information. The third guideline’s concern is with the plan’s style and format. The plan’s appearance should be evaluated carefully; however, it should be consistent with a conventional structure and should not suggest to the potential investor that a great deal of money was spent to prepare the plan itself.

LO 6.4 A business plan has multiple parts, the first of which is the executive summary. The executive summary is a quick overview of the entire business plan and provides busy readers with everything they need to know about the distinctive nature of the new venture. In many instances, an investor will ask for a copy of a firm’s executive summary and will request a copy of the full business plan only when the executive summary is convincing. The industry analysis, which is another part of the business plan, describes the industry a business will enter. The market analysis part of the plan breaks the industry analysis into segments and zeroes in on the specific segments (or target markets) to which the firm will seek to appeal. The management team and company structure section of a business plan are critical. Many investors and others who read business plans look first at the executive summary and then go directly to the management team section to assess the strength of the people starting the firm. The sources and uses of funds statement is a document that lays out specifically how much money a firm will need (if it is raising money), where the money will come from and its use. An assumptions sheet explains the most critical assumptions used to complete a firm’s financial analysis.

LO 6.5 After writing the business plan, the entrepreneur must prepare to present it effectively to potential investors and possibly others as well. The oral presentation of a business plan typically consists of 20 minutes of formal remarks, accompanied by approximately 12 PowerPoint slides and 40 minutes of questions and answers. The presentation should be smooth and well rehearsed. The slides should be sharp and not cluttered with material.


CASE 6.1 Two Startups That Tackled the Same Problem—Helping People Find a Place to Store Their Stuff: One Business Plan Failed While the Other Is Leading to the Firm’s Success
Neighbor: Web: www.neighbor.com • Facebook: Neighbor • Instagram: @tryneighbor

Bruce R. Barringer, Oklahoma State University

R. Duane Ireland, Texas A&M University

Introduction
A common problem people have is needing storage—whether it is for short- or long-term purposes. Often, people need temporary storage when they move. For example, a person’s new job might begin on April 1 while the house or apartment into which they are moving will be available on June 1. People with excess possessions or who have a seasonal item such as an RV or boat that they use primarily during the summer months, causing them to need a storage option during winter months, require storage capabilities on something more than a temporary basis. The $29 billion self-storage industry exists to solve these problems. Almost every town in the United States and many communities in other nations as well have one or more self-storage companies. Less Mess, for example, claims to be the most popular self-storage company in Poland and the Czech Republic while Giba Self-Storage serves South Africa. According to SpareFoot, the go-to source for news and data on the U.S. self-storage industry, there are over 51,200 self-storage facilities in the country with 11.1 percent of U.S. households renting a self-storage unit.

As with all industries, the self-storage industry is not without issues. As examples, some people complain that there is not a self-storage facility close to where they live, that the prices are too high, and that transporting items to and from the storage facility is inconvenient. Additionally, because self-storage units charge by square foot, there is an incentive for users to maximize the number of items they place in a storage unit. That makes items hard to find and hard to access, limiting personal storage to items that people use infrequently.

As illustrated throughout this text, problems cause entrepreneurs to take notice, and this is the case in the self-storage industry. The following is a brief recap of two startups that tackled the same problem: how to disrupt the self-storage industry and provide customers with a better experience. Both companies raised substantial funding ($35.3 million for the one that failed and $65.7 million and counting for the other that is succeeding). While these two firms set out to tackle the same problem, their business plans to do so differed substantially. Indeed, one business plan did not lead to firm success while the other one did.

Omni
Omni started in 2015 with the goal of providing its customers with an improved storage solution. Launched in San Francisco, the firm’s business plan had two core elements, the first one being storage itself. For those needing storage, Onmi would send a van to your house, pick up the items you needed stored, drive them to a warehouse, store them, and bring them back whenever needed. Instead of charging on a square-foot basis (which is the procedure most self-storage facilities follow), Omni charged on a per-item basis ($0.50/month for small items and $3.00/month for large ones). The firm photographed and labeled each of a customer’s items; this information was then searchable from within the Omni app.

To get customers, the firm focused on two groups, the first one being collectors—people who have more possessions than space to store them. Omni’s interest was in customer items that were of relatively high quality that people did not need to use daily. What was particularly attractive about this customer segment is that some collectors had over 1,000 items to store, which provided good income for Omni. The second group were apartment dwellers. Omni would partner with local apartment complexes and hold events in their lobbies promoting its service. The firm would complete onsite check-ins, where people could bring items to them on the spot (that they might have stuffed into an apartment closet), and they would take the items and store the items for them. Omni also felt that trust was a big part of what it was selling. As a result, when its vans arrived at a customer’s home, they had the Omni logo on the side and the employees all wore Omni uniforms.

The second element of Omni’s business plan was to allow people who had items stored to rent their items to others. It is unclear if Omni envisioned offering this service from the beginning or whether this idea surfaced later. Either way, they waited until they felt that they had sufficient items in storage to provide enough items for a rental marketplace to be attractive. Omni made money by taking a percentage of the rental income their customers earned. Interestingly, Omni found that once a customer had at least three of their items rented, they started to store more items (presumably hoping that more of their items would be rented), which increased Omni’s income on the storage side. This left Omni in an enviable position in that the more items a customer stored, the more items that were rented, and the more items that were rented the more items that were stored. In addition, Omni felt it had an ideal situation regarding its marketplace. If a house is listed on Airbnb and is never rented, the owner will eventually remove the listing and use the house for something else. In this instance, Airbnb makes nothing. If an item on the Omni marketplace never gets rented, the person who owns the items will likely keep them in storage, and Omni will continue to make money on the storage side.

In the end, Omni’s business plan did not work. While they offered a premier service to their customers, it relied on thin margins to earn profits. Just think of what took place on the storage side. To make its service work, Omni had to buy delivery vans, pay drivers, plan delivery routes, deal with traffic, fuel and maintain delivery vans, index and photograph what their customers wanted stored, upload the photos to an app, and provide the labor needed to load their customers’ belongings and unload them at a warehouse. If a customer wanted something that was in storage, Omni would retrieve it and drive it to the customer’s house. In addition, they had to find and rent large warehouses to store their customers’ items. That is a lot of effort and expense for 50 cents a month for small items and $3 a month for large ones. Omni’s weakness is the storage area was not covered sufficiently by revenues from its rental business, and the company shut down. Omni hoped that volume would eventually make its business profitable; however, this desired outcome was not reached in time to prevent the firm’s failure.

Omni tried several pivots before closing its operations. It sold off its storage operations to Clutter, one of its competitors, in May 2019. Omni then refocused by trying to help retail stores rent items such as tools and tents. Doing this did not lead to success because it required too big of a behavioral change for store owners and their customers while still relying on slim margins.

Neighbor
Neighbor, started by BYU graduates Joseph Woodbury, Preston Alder, and Colton Gardner, launched in early 2017. As with Omni, Neighbor felt that the self-storage industry was ripe for disruption and that the firm could provide a better solution for customers. Neighbor operates a peer-to-peer marketplace where it connects “renters,” who are people in need of storage space, with “hosts,” who are people in their neighborhoods or towns who are willing to lease space. Neighbor’s market, which includes both individuals and businesses, earns money by taking a commission on each match it makes between a renter and a host.

Here is how Neighbor works. If you need some storage space, you log onto Neighbor’s website. The categories of storage that are normally available include self-storage (the kind of items you would store in a storage unit), car storage, RV storage, boat storage, trailer storage, business inventory, and fleet parking. You enter a location, which can be your address or the town or city in which you live. If you type in your address, Neighbor then displays for you all the rentals available near your location. Neighbor then shows you an approximate location but, like Airbnb, the exact address comes to you only when you reserve the space. You will also see the amenities the space provides. For example, the

Like Omni, Neighbor recognizes that trust is the most significant barrier to someone using its service. Neighbor charges 4.9 percent of the monthly rate a host receives, along with a $0.30 fee for each reservation you book. So, if you rent an extra bedroom (strictly for storage) for $100/month, Neighbor will take $5.20 from the amount your renter pays you, meaning that you will net $94.80 before taxes. Neighbor’s service is available in all 50 U.S. states. According to TechCrunch, Neighbor claims that the self-storage offered on its marketplace carries a price that is about 40 to 50 percent less, on average, than traditional storage facilities. Unlike many self-storage facilities, Neighbor also makes it possible for a renter to not only find storage space for extra belongings but for RVs, boats, cars, trailers, business inventory, and more.

Neighbor is based in Utah and has raised $65.7 million from investors. The firm’s 53 million dollar Series B round, which took place in spring 2021, is reportedly the largest Series B round raised by a Utah company. Neighbor has ambitious growth plans. Along with increasing its listings in cities and towns across the United States, it also partners with commercial real estate companies to convert the underutilized or vacant space in their inventory into self-storage. This provides new revenue streams to retail and commercial property owners.

CASE 6.2 d.light: How Bringing Its Business Plan to Life Helped a Social Enterprise Get Off to a Strong Start
Web: www.dlight.com • Facebook: d.light Design • Twitter: @dlightdesign

Bruce R. Barringer, Oklahoma State University

R. Duane Ireland, Texas A&M University

Introduction
Imagine the following. You are in the audience of a business plan competition. The next team to present is d.light, a for-profit social enterprise that plans to bring light to people without access to reliable electricity. Two young men introduce themselves as the cofounders of d.light, and indicate that they will begin their presentation with a demonstration. The lights go out. In a few seconds, you see a dim light at the front of the room, and smell smoke and burning kerosene. After about 30 seconds, your eyes start to water, and it becomes slightly uncomfortable to breathe. The lights switch back on and the smoke clears. The young men apologize for the lack of light and the smoke but say that they staged the demonstration to illustrate a point. Around 1.2 billion people, or more than one-fifth of the world’s population, have no access to electricity, and about a billion more have an unreliable or intermittent supply. An estimated 500 million households must burn some sort of fuel, such as kerosene, for cooking or lighting. Kerosene fuel is expensive, dangerous, and toxic to both people and the environment. In fact, the United Nations estimates that kerosene fumes kill 1.5 million people annually and cause countless health complications for others.

Sam Goldman and the Origins of d.light
The scene described here actually took place—several times. It is how Sam Goldman and Ned Tozun, d.light’s cofounders, introduced the company at business plan competitions and when pitching to investors. d.light is an international consumer products company serving “base of the pyramid” consumers without access to reliable electricity. Although d.light technically started in a class at Stanford University, we can trace its beginning to Sam Goldman’s youth and early adulthood. Growing up, Goldman’s parents worked for the United States Agency for International Development, a government agency that provides economic and humanitarian assistance in countries across the globe. Goldman lived in Pakistan, Peru, India, Canada, and several other countries. As a young adult, while working for the Peace Corps, he lived for four years in a West African village without electricity. A neighbor boy was burned badly in a kerosene fire, an event that impacted Goldman deeply. At one point during his time in the village, Goldman was given a battery-powered LED headlamp and was struck by the dramatic difference that simply having light at night can make in a person’s life. He could now cook, read, and do things at night that were unimaginable without the benefits reliable lighting provides.

Impacted by this experience, Goldman sought out a graduate program that would provide an opportunity for him to start thinking about creating a business to take light to people without access to reliable electricity. He landed at Stanford, which was starting a program in social enterprise. A pivotal class was Jim Patelli’s 2006–2007 Entrepreneurial Design course. Class members formed teams, with each team receiving a challenge to address a significant issue in low-income countries such as Burma. Goldman teamed up with Ned Tozun, a business classmate, and two engineering students, Erica Estrade and Xian Wu. The team tackled the problem of light for people without access to reliable electricity and developed a rough prototype of a portable LED light that solar power could recharge. That spring, the team traveled to Burma to go into villages that lacked access to electricity to introduce their device. Villagers told them they spent up to 40 percent of their income on kerosene. When shown how their crude prototype could provide light at night and be recharged during the day simply by deploying small solar panels on their homes, the villagers were so taken, one woman started weeping with joy. According to one account of the team’s trip, in one village the local police confiscated the prototypes. They, too, needed light at night.

Design and Distribution
After completing the Entrepreneurial Design course, the teammates headed in separate directions for the summer. In the fall, they reunited, determined to continue working on their business concept. The concept of using solar power to recharge portable lights in poor rural areas was not a new one. In fact, it had been tried many times. The problem, in Goldman and his team’s view, was a combination of design and distribution. Previous models relied either on nongovernmental organizations (NGOs) and governments “giving” expensive lights to people without access to electricity, which they then could not afford to replace when used up or if broken, or commercial enterprises buying extremely inexpensive lights in China and exporting them to Africa and elsewhere, where they performed poorly. It was clear to Goldman that neither of these models was sustainable.

So, Goldman and his team, driven by the possibility of changing literally millions of people’s lives throughout the world, recruited talented engineers and distribution experts, who worked on a near pro bono basis, to help with the project. The goal was to produce a solar-powered portable LED light that was exactly what rural villagers needed—nothing more and nothing less. It also had to be cheap enough that villagers could afford it yet capable of being produced in a way that yielded sufficient margins for d.light to be profitable. The decision was made early on that d.light would be a for-profit company. The company’s goal was not to impact 100,000 people or a million people but to impact hundreds of millions of people. Goldman and his team knew that their lofty ambitions would take cash and additional research and development efforts, which would require private-sector investment capital.

During this period, which covered the summer of 2007 until early 2008, Goldman and his cofounders continued traveling to remote areas to obtain feedback about their prototype. The team thinned some in early 2008, with Goldman, Tozun, and Wu continuing. d.light was now up and running and opened its first international offices in India, China, and Tanzania.

Business Plan Competitions and Investor Presentations
One thing Goldman stresses during talks about d.light is the instrumental role the company’s business plan played in helping the company take shape and in raising investment capital. Early on, d.light entered several business plan competitions. In spring 2007, it took second place in the University of California, Berkeley Social Venture Competition and won first prize at Stanford’s Social E-Challenge. A big breakthrough happened in May 2007, when the team claimed the $250,000 first prize in the prestigious Draper Fisher Jurvetson Venture Challenge competition. This money provided seed funding for much of the work the firm completed during the summer and fall of 2007.

What is particularly interesting is Goldman’s reflections about why d.light was so successful in business plan competitions and eventually with investor presentations. These reflections are instructive for entrepreneurs as they think about how to design and then establish their entrepreneurial ventures successfully. As shown in the table below, there are six reasons that account for d.light’s success, specifically with business plan competitions. Collectively, the attributes we show in the table present d.light as an organization with a compelling idea, a strong management team, large markets to serve, an intense product focus, and a coherent, resolute, and extremely admirable vision for the future.

d.light Today
Today, d.light is having the impact that its cofounders envisioned. Its stated goal is to transform the lives of 1 billion people by 2030. The chart below shows the numbers on d.light’s social impact dashboard, which is updated frequently and posted on the company’s website. These numbers reflect the impact of the availability of light, produced by d.light solar lanterns, for people who previously lacked access to reliable electricity and light. The numbers are remarkable, particularly in terms of lives empowered, school-age children reached with solar lighting, and savings in energy-related expenses. The numbers reflect the good that a well-managed social enterprise can create.

Six Key Reasons d.light Was Successful in Business Plan Competitions
Told Stories and Showed Pictures

While many teams enter business plan competitions with impressive PowerPoint slides and bullet points, d.light focused on telling stories and showing pictures. The company’s cofounders showed photographs of rural villagers using their device and shared their testimonials. In one interview, Goldman remarked that no matter how many plans or pitches a group of judges or investors heard during a day, he was confident they remembered d.light’s presentation.

All In

The cofounders were committed totally to d.light. They passed on corporate jobs and focused on d.light full time. Along with “talking the talk,” they also “walked the walk.” Instead of saying that they planned to travel to remote villages to test their device, they just went out and did it. Some of the trips came before the business plan presentations.

Right Team

d.light put together a strong team, with a balance of business and engineering expertise. The team was well suited for launching a global initiative. Goldman lived in many countries during his life, including India and Pakistan. Other team members showed too that they had no inhibitions about traveling to remote villages to talk about their device.

Big Market

d.light was tackling a large market, which investors like. To make their financials work, the team would have to scale the business and sell millions of units. While the challenge was great, so was the potential payoff.

Product Focus

The company iterated its device multiple times before settling on its first solar-powered portable LED light, called the Nova. The Nova sold for the U.S. equivalent of around $25. Early feedback indicated that the price was still too high, so more iterations took place to find ways to reduce the product’s price further.

Strong Vision

Although d.light was a social enterprise, it unashamedly presented itself as a for-profit venture. The team was resolute that getting to scale was possible only via private-sector capital. Additionally, d.light chose to measure its success by the number of families on which it had a positive impact. This sense of purpose and vision permeated the organization.

d.light sells its products in 62 countries through five distribution hubs in East Africa, West Africa, India, Southeast Asia, and the United States. One strategy that works well for the company is to employ “rural entrepreneurs” to sell the product. d.light likes to employ Indigenous personnel—who know the local customs, people, and language—to sell its product on a commission basis. It also has partnerships with NGOs, microfinance organizations, and social enterprise startups that are producing solar lanterns to achieve the same goals that d.light strives to reach. d.light funds its operations and growth through both investment capital and earnings. The firm updates its existing products continuously while taking actions to develop new ones. For example, the firm now sells a suite of solar lanterns, solar home systems, solar appliances, and portable chargers.

Looking Ahead
As it continues to grow, the problem d.light is trying to solve, to provide reliable sources of light to people without access to electricity, is as large as ever. Incredibly, the number of people worldwide without access to reliable electricity is larger today than it was when d.light launched. The challenge for the firm is to continue its current operations while determining additional paths to pursue to increase its product’s impact on people’s lives.

d.light’s efforts have clearly made a difference in the lives of millions of people. The firm has received numerous awards to recognize its accomplishments. One particularly nice distinction is its place in the British Museum’s History of the World in 100 Objects. d.light’s basic lantern, the S250, is object number 100 in the collection.
