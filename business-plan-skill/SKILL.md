# Business Plan — Comprehensive Skill

## 1. Overview & Philosophy

A business plan is not a static document you write once and file away. It is a **living strategy document** — a forcing function that makes you think systematically through every aspect of your venture and a reference you update as reality proves or disproves your assumptions.

### 1.1 Why Write a Business Plan

There are two primary reasons:

1. **Internal Reason — Clarity of Thought**: Writing a business plan forces the founding team to think through every dimension of the business — market, competition, operations, team, finances — in a disciplined, systematic way. This process typically takes days to weeks and reveals blind spots, untested assumptions, and strategic gaps that casual thinking misses. The process of writing is as valuable as the document itself.

2. **External Reason — Stakeholder Communication**: The plan communicates the venture's merits to outside stakeholders — investors, lenders, potential partners, key employees, and advisors. It demonstrates that the founders have done their homework and can articulate a compelling, evidence-based case for the business.

> **Statistic**: Only 33% of business owners have a formal business plan (Wells Fargo/Gallup Small Business Index Survey). However, businesses WITH plans have significantly higher expectations for employment growth, revenue growth, and capital spending. Writing a plan is a competitive advantage.

### 1.2 Three Plan Types

Select the plan type based on the venture's stage, audience, and purpose. The agent should auto-select the appropriate type based on the user's context, or ask if ambiguous.

| Plan Type | Pages | When to Use | Audience |
|-----------|-------|-------------|----------|
| **Summary Plan** | 10–15 | Very early-stage ideas; experienced entrepreneurs testing a new concept; initial outreach to investors who want a quick read | Seed investors, accelerators, internal clarity |
| **Full Business Plan** | 25–35 | Most common format; formal investor presentations; bank loans; partner negotiations; competition submissions | VCs, angels, banks, partners, competition judges |
| **Operational Plan** | 40–100 | Internal blueprint for operations; detailed department-level plans; post-funding execution guide | Internal management team, functional department heads |

### 1.3 When to Plan vs. When to Iterate

The Lean Startup movement advocates experimentation over formal planning. This skill takes a **both-and** position: a business plan preceded by feasibility analysis is an essential starting point, but it must NOT be static. Use the plan as a decision-making framework, then iterate based on customer feedback, market data, and real-world results. Update the plan monthly for the first 6 months, quarterly after that.

### 1.4 The Planning Pipeline

The business plan is the **culmination** of prior strategic work, not the starting point:

```
Feasibility Analysis → Business Model Canvas → Competitive Analysis → Business Plan
```

If the user has not completed prior analyses, this skill self-contains the necessary frameworks. If companion skills exist (market-research, competitive-analysis, positioning-strategy, business-model-canvas), pull data from their outputs.

## 2. Interactive Interview Protocol

Before generating the plan, conduct a structured interview to gather the user's inputs. This protocol ensures the plan is grounded in the founder's actual knowledge, not generic boilerplate.

### Phase 1 — Core Identity (5 Questions)

Ask these questions one at a time, adapting follow-ups based on answers:

1. **What does your business do?** — Ask for a one-sentence description. If the answer is longer than two sentences, help them distill it. A clear one-liner is the foundation of every plan section.

2. **What problem does it solve, and for whom?** — Push for concrete detail: Who feels this pain? When? How often? What does it cost them (time, money, stress)? Ask for evidence: customer quotes, forum posts, market data. If they say "everyone needs this," challenge them — the best businesses serve a specific audience exceptionally well.

3. **What stage are you at?** — Categorize:
   - **Idea Stage**: Concept only, no product, no revenue
   - **Pre-Revenue**: Product in development or MVP built, no paying customers yet
   - **Early Revenue**: First customers acquired, product-market fit being validated
   - **Growing**: Repeatable sales process, scaling operations
   - **Scaling**: Proven model, optimizing for growth efficiency
   
   This determines the plan type (Summary vs. Full vs. Operational) and the appropriate level of financial detail.

4. **What is your legal structure and location?** — Sole proprietorship, LLC, S-Corp, C-Corp, or undecided? Where do you operate — remote, local, hybrid, multi-market? If undecided on legal structure, flag for the Legal Structure Advisory section.

5. **What is your business name and founding/launch date?** — If pre-launch, what is the planned launch date?

### Phase 2 — Market & Competition (5 Questions)

6. **Who is your target customer?** — Ask for specifics across multiple segmentation dimensions:
   - **Demographics**: Age, gender, income, education, occupation, family status
   - **Psychographics**: Values, attitudes, lifestyle, interests
   - **Behavioral**: Usage patterns, benefits sought, purchase frequency, brand loyalty
   - **Geographic**: City, region, country, urban vs. rural
   
   If the user serves multiple segments, ask them to rank by priority and identify their **primary beachhead segment** — the one they'll dominate first.

7. **How large is your market?** — Guide them through market sizing:
   - **TAM** (Total Addressable Market): The total revenue opportunity if 100% of the market adopted your solution
   - **SAM** (Serviceable Addressable Market): The portion of TAM within your geographic and segment reach
   - **SOM** (Serviceable Obtainable Market): The realistic share you can capture in 1–3 years
   
   If they don't know, use the Four Methods for Estimating Sales (see Section 3.6) to help them calculate.

8. **Who are your top 3–5 competitors?** — For each competitor, ask: What do they do well? What do they do poorly? What is their pricing? How do they acquire customers? Include direct competitors (same solution), indirect competitors (different solution to same problem), and potential future competitors.

9. **What is your competitive wedge?** — The specific position you occupy that competitors don't. This is not a feature list — it's a strategic position. Examples: "We're the only X that also does Y," "We serve a segment that everyone else ignores," "We're 10x cheaper because of Z."

10. **Why now?** — What has changed in technology, regulation, customer behavior, or market dynamics that makes this the right time? This is critical for investors — they want to know why this hasn't been done before and what makes now different.

### Phase 3 — Revenue & Operations (5 Questions)

11. **How do customers find you?** — List all acquisition channels: organic search, paid ads, social media, referrals, partnerships, content marketing, outbound sales, events, app stores. Which 2–3 will you focus on first and why?

12. **How do customers buy?** — Describe the sales flow from first touch to closed deal:
    - Discovery → Evaluation → Purchase → Onboarding → Retention
    - Is it self-serve (checkout page), sales-assisted (demo → proposal → close), or enterprise (RFP → POC → contract)?

13. **What do you charge and how?** — Revenue model specifics:
    - Pricing model: subscription, one-time, freemium, marketplace commission, licensing, usage-based
    - Price points: What do you charge? How did you arrive at that price?
    - If multiple revenue streams, list each with its pricing

14. **What does fulfillment look like?** — From purchase to outcome: How does a customer go from paying you to getting value? What is the delivery mechanism? What is the timeline?

15. **What tools and technology do you use (or plan to use)?** — Technology stack, platforms, infrastructure, key software. Include anything critical to operations.

### Phase 4 — Financials & Risk (5 Questions)

16. **What are your startup costs (or current monthly burn rate)?** — List all one-time startup costs (legal, licenses, equipment, website, inventory) and recurring monthly costs. If already operating, what is the current monthly burn?

17. **What are your fixed vs. variable costs?** — Fixed costs: rent, salaries, software subscriptions, insurance (don't change with sales volume). Variable costs: materials, shipping, transaction fees, commissions (scale with sales volume).

18. **What funding do you need and from whom?** — Amount sought, source (bootstrapping, friends/family, angel investors, VCs, bank loans, grants, crowdfunding), intended use of funds, and what you'll give in return (equity %, interest rate, nothing).

19. **What are the top 3 risks to this business?** — Prompt with common risk categories if they struggle:
    - Single-person dependency
    - Platform/API dependency
    - Customer concentration
    - Market timing (too early or too late)
    - Pricing risk
    - Competition from well-funded players
    - Regulatory changes
    - Supply chain disruption
    - Technology obsolescence

20. **What is your 12-month revenue target?** — And the math behind it: How many customers × what price × what conversion rate = that number?

### Interview Completion

After all four phases, summarize the user's answers back to them for confirmation before generating the plan. Ask: "Is there anything you'd like to correct or add before I generate your business plan?"

## 3. Plan Generation Engine — Section-by-Section Playbook

Generate each section using the user's interview answers, applying the frameworks and standards below. For a **Summary Plan**, condense each section to its essentials. For a **Full Plan**, follow the detailed guidance below. For an **Operational Plan**, expand each section with department-level detail and operational procedures.

### 3.1 Cover Page & Table of Contents

**Generate:**
- Company name and logo (if provided)
- Company address, phone, email, website, social media handles
- Date of the plan
- Lead entrepreneur/CEO name and contact information
- Confidentiality notice: *"This document contains confidential information. Do not distribute without written permission."*
- Version number (v1.0 for new plans)

**Table of Contents**: Auto-generate from section headings with page references.

### 3.2 Executive Summary

> **CRITICAL RULE**: Write this section LAST, after all other sections are complete. It is a distillation of the entire plan, not an introduction.

**Structure** (maximum 2 single-spaced pages):

1. **What the business does** — One sentence. Crystal clear.
2. **The problem it solves and for whom** — Two sentences maximum. Quantify the pain.
3. **The solution and why it's different** — Two sentences. Include the core innovation.
4. **The market opportunity** — One sentence with a number (TAM or SOM).
5. **Business model** — One sentence on how you make money.
6. **Traction** — One sentence on milestones achieved (if any).
7. **The team** — One sentence on why this team is uniquely qualified.
8. **What you're asking for** — One sentence: funding amount, partnership, or plan of action.

**Generate TWO versions**: One embedded in the plan, one standalone (for sending ahead of meetings).

**Quality Test**: If someone reads ONLY the executive summary, they should understand the entire business. If they want details, the rest of the plan delivers them.

### 3.3 Industry Analysis

This section analyzes the **industry** (not the specific target market — that's Section 3.6).

**Generate these subsections:**

- **Industry Size & Growth**: Current market size (revenue), historical growth rate, projected growth. Cite sources.
- **Industry Structure**: Is the industry fragmented (many small players, more receptive to new entrants) or concentrated (few large players, higher barriers to entry)?
- **Nature of Participants**: Are existing players innovative or conservative? Quick or slow to react to market changes? Are they profitable?
- **Key Success Factors**: Identify 6–10 factors that all participants must master. Then identify the 2–3 where the venture will differentiate.
- **Industry Trends**: Categorize by type:
  - Economic trends (pricing pressure, margin compression/expansion)
  - Social trends (changing demographics, shifting preferences)
  - Technological trends (emerging tech, platform shifts, AI impact)
  - Political/regulatory trends (new laws, deregulation, compliance requirements)
- **Long-Term Prospects**: Is this industry growing, stable, or declining? What external forces could accelerate or disrupt it?

### 3.4 Company Description

**Generate:**

- **Company History / Origin Story**: If the founding story is heartfelt or demonstrates deep domain knowledge, tell it. Stories like "I experienced this problem firsthand" are more compelling than corporate language. Reference: LuminAid — inflatable solar LED light inspired by the 2010 Haiti earthquake.
- **Mission Statement**: Define why the company exists and what it aspires to become. This serves as both a financial and moral compass. Keep it under 30 words.
- **Tagline**: A memorable phrase that captures the brand essence (e.g., Good Catch's "Seafood Evolved").
- **Products/Services**: What the company offers, how it's unique, and market positioning.
- **Current Status**: Frame in terms of milestones achieved (product built, beta launched, first customers acquired, revenue milestones, team hired, partnerships secured).
- **Legal Status & Ownership**: Form of business, ownership split, equity structure.
- **Key Partnerships**: Strategic relationships that strengthen the business.

### 3.5 Problem & Solution

This is the **heart of the plan**. If this section is weak, everything after it is built on sand.

**3.5.1 The Problem**
- Describe the specific problem with concrete detail. Who feels it? When? How often?
- What does it cost them (time, money, stress, risk)?
- Back it up with evidence: customer discovery quotes, forum posts, survey data, market research. Not just your opinion.
- Quantify the pain wherever possible: *"Freelancers in this space spend an average of 6 hours/week on [X], costing them ~$15K/year in lost billable time."*

**3.5.2 The Solution**
- Describe what your product or service does. Be specific about the **user experience** — not just features, but what happens from the customer's perspective.
- Paint the **Before & After**: Before = the painful status quo. After = what life looks like with your solution.
- Identify the **core innovation or insight** — the non-obvious thing that makes your solution work where others have failed or haven't tried.

**3.5.3 Why Now**
- What has changed (technology, regulation, market behavior, customer expectations) that creates this window of opportunity?
- Why hasn't this been done before? What's different now?
- This is critical for investor credibility — timing is one of the top predictors of startup success.

### 3.6 Market Analysis

This section narrows from the broad industry to your **specific target market**.

**3.6.1 Market Segmentation**

Segment the market using one or more dimensions:
- **Geographic**: Country, region, city, urban/suburban/rural, climate
- **Demographic**: Age, gender, income, education, occupation, family size
- **Psychographic**: Values, attitudes, lifestyle, personality, social class
- **Behavioral**: Usage rate, benefits sought, loyalty status, purchase occasion

Apply **multi-dimensional segmentation** for precision. Example: Lively segmented the cell phone market by both age (older adults) AND benefits sought (simplicity, large buttons, emergency features).

**3.6.2 Buyer Behavior**

Describe the purchase decision process for your target segment:
- Who is the decision-maker? (Individual, committee, household?)
- What triggers a purchase? (Pain event, seasonal, lifecycle change?)
- What is the evaluation process? (Comparison shopping, referral-based, impulse?)
- What are the barriers to purchase? (Price sensitivity, trust, switching costs, inertia?)

**3.6.3 Market Sizing — Four Methods**

Use at least two methods to triangulate market size estimates:

| Method | How It Works | Best For |
|--------|-------------|----------|
| **Multiplication Method (Top-Down)** | Total potential users × average price × estimated market share % | Broad markets with published data |
| **Multiplication Method (Bottom-Up)** | Estimated customers you can realistically reach × average spend per customer | Niche markets, local businesses |
| **Comparable Firm Analysis** | Find a similar business and benchmark against their revenue at a similar stage | When direct market data is scarce |
| **Industry Trade Association Data** | Contact trade associations for rules of thumb: "average sales per square foot," "average sales per employee" | Brick-and-mortar, service businesses |

**Generate TAM/SAM/SOM calculations** with clear methodology and sources for each number.

| Target segment | | | | |

### 3.7 Competitive Landscape

Expand beyond the matrix with strategic analysis:

- **Competitive Wedge**: The specific position you occupy that competitors don't. This is your "why us" answer.
- **Table Stakes**: Things you must do as well as everyone else (hygiene factors). Don't differentiate on these — just match them.
- **Gaps You Fill**: Things competitors miss that you solve. These are your differentiation opportunities.
- **Honest Competitor Strengths**: Pretending competitors are weak makes your plan LESS credible, not more. Name their real strengths and explain specifically why you win anyway.
- **Potential Future Competitors**: Who could enter this space? What would trigger them?

### 3.8 Economics of the Business

This section demonstrates that the business model is financially viable.

**3.8.1 Revenue Drivers**

List every way the business earns money. For each:
- Description of the revenue stream
- Pricing model and price point
- Expected contribution to total revenue (%)

**3.8.2 Margin Analysis**

For each revenue driver, calculate:
- **Gross Margin** = (Selling Price − COGS or Variable Costs) ÷ Selling Price
- **Contribution Margin** = Selling Price − Variable Cost Per Unit (amount available to cover fixed costs and produce profit)

If multiple revenue drivers exist, calculate the **Weighted Average Contribution Margin**:
```
WACM = Σ (Contribution Margin_i × Revenue Share_i)
```

**3.8.3 Cost Structure**

| Cost Category | Fixed/Variable | Monthly Amount | Annual Amount |
|---------------|---------------|---------------|---------------|
| [List each cost] | Fixed or Variable | $ | $ |
| **Total Fixed Costs** | | $ | $ |
| **Total Variable Costs (at target volume)** | | $ | $ |

**3.8.4 Operating Leverage**

Assess the business's operating leverage:
- **High operating leverage** (high fixed costs, low variable costs): Longer to break-even, but more profit per unit after break-even. Examples: SaaS, manufacturing.
- **Low operating leverage** (low fixed costs, high variable costs): Faster to break-even, but margins stay relatively flat. Examples: consulting, services.

**3.8.5 Startup Costs**

| One-Time Cost | Amount |
|---------------|--------|
| Legal fees (incorporation, contracts) | $ |
| Licenses and permits | $ |
| Website / app development | $ |
| Logo and branding | $ |
| Initial inventory | $ |
| Equipment / hardware | $ |
| Office setup | $ |
| Marketing launch budget | $ |
| Insurance deposits | $ |
| **Total Startup Costs** | **$** |

**3.8.6 Break-Even Analysis**

Calculate the break-even point in units and revenue:

```
Break-Even Units = Total Fixed Costs ÷ Contribution Margin per Unit
Break-Even Revenue = Total Fixed Costs ÷ Gross Margin %
```

State clearly: *"The business must sell [X] units per month (or generate $[Y] in monthly revenue) to cover all costs."*

### 3.9 Business & Revenue Model

Translate the business model into narrative form:

- **Customer Acquisition Strategy**: How customers find you (channels) and the cost of acquisition (CAC) per channel.
- **Sales Flow**: Discovery → Evaluation → Purchase → Onboarding → Retention. Describe each step.
- **Revenue Streams**: What you charge, how (subscription/one-time/commission/licensing), and at what price points.
- **Revenue Projections**: Monthly for Year 1, Quarterly for Years 2–3. Use bottom-up math:
  *"If I acquire [X] customers per month at [Y] price with [Z]% conversion rate, revenue = [result]."*
- **Unit Economics**:
  - **CAC** (Customer Acquisition Cost): Total marketing spend ÷ new customers acquired
  - **LTV** (Lifetime Value): Average revenue per customer × average customer lifetime
  - **LTV:CAC Ratio**: Should be ≥ 3:1 for a healthy business
  - **Payback Period**: Months to recover CAC from a single customer's revenue

### 3.10 Marketing & Sales Plan

Keep this section to 4–5 pages in the plan.

- **Positioning Statement**: [From positioning-strategy skill if available, or generate:] *"For [target customer], [company name] is the [category] that [key benefit] because [reason to believe]."*
- **Marketing Strategy**: Overall approach — inbound vs. outbound, brand-led vs. performance-led, community-driven vs. paid acquisition.
- **The 4Ps**:
  - **Product**: Core offering, features, packaging, product roadmap
  - **Price**: Pricing strategy (penetration, skimming, value-based, competitive), consistency with positioning
  - **Promotions**: Content marketing, paid advertising, PR, partnerships, events, email, social media
  - **Place/Distribution**: How the product reaches the customer (direct, marketplace, retail, wholesale, online)
- **Content Strategy**: What content you'll create, how often, on which platforms, targeting which audience
- **Sales Process/Cycle**: Don't just describe strategy — describe HOW you'll actually sell. What is the sales conversation? What objections do you handle? What is the close process?
- **Customer Acquisition Targets**: How many customers per month to hit revenue goals
- **CAC Budget**: How much you'll spend per acquired customer, by channel

> **Price-Positioning Rule**: A low price on a "premium quality" product confuses the market. Price must be consistent with your positioning and the value delivered.

### 3.11 Design & Development Plan

**3.11.1 Development Status & Stages**

Map the current position on the development lifecycle:
```
Conception → Prototyping → Initial Production → Full Production
```

- **Prototyping**: Physical prototypes or virtual prototypes (using CAD/CAE software for hardware, wireframes/mockups for software).
- State what has been completed and what remains.

**3.11.2 Challenges & Risks**

Be honest — painting an overly rosy picture destroys credibility. List:
- Technical challenges and how you plan to overcome them
- Resource constraints and workarounds
- Dependencies on third parties

**3.11.3 Projected Development Costs**

Timeline and budget for remaining development milestones.

**3.11.4 Intellectual Property**

- **Patents**: Filed, pending, or planned? Provisional or utility?
- **Trademarks**: Registered or planned?
- **Copyrights**: What original works are protected?
- **Trade Secrets**: What proprietary knowledge exists?
- **Recommendation**: Get legal counsel early on IP strategy.

### 3.12 Operations Plan

Keep this to 3–4 pages. Use the **Back Stage / Front Stage** framework:

**Back Stage** (what the customer doesn't see):
- Staff selection and training processes
- Operations manual and SOPs
- Supplier and vendor relationships
- Government compliance and regulatory requirements
- Marketing material production
- Emergency and contingency plans
- Technology infrastructure management

**Front Stage** (what the customer experiences):
- Service delivery and customer interaction
- Operating hours and availability
- Customer support process
- Product/content delivery
- Community engagement
- Onboarding experience

**Additional Operations Elements:**
- **Business Location**: Is location critical to the business (e.g., retail, logistics) or less important (e.g., SaaS, consulting)?
- **Facilities & Equipment**: Purchase, lease, or outsource? Cost implications of each.
- **Key Processes**: The 3–5 recurring processes that keep the business running (e.g., customer onboarding, invoice generation, content publishing, order fulfillment, customer support).
- **Automation Plan**: Which processes can be automated, with what tools, and in what order?
- **Outsourcing Plan**: Which tasks will you eventually delegate? What triggers the decision (revenue threshold, time constraint, skill gap)?

### 3.13 Management Team & Company Structure

> **Critical Note**: Investors often read the executive summary, then jump directly to the management team section. The perception that one team is better prepared than another often determines funding decisions — not just the idea.

**For each team member, generate a profile:**
- Name and title
- Key responsibilities
- Relevant industry experience
- Previous successes / track record
- Educational background
- What they bring to this specific venture

**Highlight:**
- **Previous working relationships** among team members (signals trust and reduced execution risk)
- **Complementary skills**: Business + technical + domain expertise balance

**Board of Directors vs. Board of Advisors:**
- **Board of Directors**: Legal fiduciary responsibility, formal governance
- **Board of Advisors**: Non-binding guidance, fills competency gaps, provides credibility and network access

**Gap Analysis:**
- Identify skill/experience gaps in the current team
- Plan and timetable for filling those gaps
- How advisors or board members compensate in the interim

**Organizational Chart**: Generate a visual hierarchy showing distribution of authority and accountability.

**3.15.7 Scenario Analysis**

Generate TWO scenarios:
- **Conservative**: Lower growth assumptions, higher costs, longer sales cycles. This scenario should still show a viable business.
- **Optimistic**: Stretch targets with everything going right.

**Key Thresholds to Calculate:**
- **Break-even point**: What monthly revenue covers all costs?
- **Runway**: How many months of operating capital before break-even?
- **Cash flow timing**: Which months do expenses spike (launch, seasonal, hiring)?

> **Honesty Rule**: Projections are educated guesses. Label them as such. Show your math. "Year 1 revenue: $500K" with no underlying logic is useless — show the customer count, price, conversion rate, and retention that produce that number.

## 4. Investor Pitch Deck Generator

When the user asks for a pitch deck, generate a **12-slide presentation outline** with content guidance for each slide:

| Slide # | Title | Content to Generate |
|---------|-------|-------------------|
| 1 | **Title Slide** | Company name, founder names, logo, tagline, contact info |
| 2 | **The Problem** | State the problem with data and emotional resonance. Make the audience feel the pain. |
| 3 | **The Solution** | How your product/service solves it. Before → After transformation. Demo or screenshot if applicable. |
| 4 | **Market Opportunity** | Target market definition, TAM/SAM/SOM, key trends driving growth. One compelling number. |
| 5 | **Technology / Product** | (Optional) Product specifics in accessible language. Architecture diagram for technical audiences. |
| 6 | **Competition** | Competitive positioning map or matrix. Your wedge. Why you win. |
| 7 | **Marketing & Sales** | Go-to-market strategy, sales process, primary customer research results, traction evidence. |
| 8 | **Management Team** | Team bios, how they came together, board members, advisory board. Highlight relevant experience. |
| 9 | **Financial Projections** | Revenue trajectory, profitability timeline, capital efficiency, key metrics (CAC, LTV, margins). |
| 10 | **Current Status** | Milestones achieved: product built, customers acquired, revenue generated, partnerships secured. |
| 11 | **Financing Sought** | Exact amount, intended use breakdown, expected timeline, terms offered. |
| 12 | **Summary & Close** | Restate strongest points, clear call to action, contact information. |

### 4.1 Oral Presentation Coaching

When preparing to present:

- **Follow time constraints exactly** — if given 20 minutes, prepare 15 minutes of content + 5 minutes buffer. Don't rush through too many slides.
- Present smoothly and well-rehearsed — but not robotic.
- Slides should be **sharp and clutter-free** — one key message per slide, large fonts, minimal text.
- Use **plain talk, avoid technical jargon** — investors evaluate business viability, not technical sophistication.
- Arrive early, bring your own equipment, have backup copies.
- Practice the Q&A — it's often more important than the presentation itself.

### 4.2 Q&A Preparation

Prepare answers for these common investor questions:
- What's your unfair advantage?
- How did you arrive at these financial projections?
- What happens if a major competitor enters your space?
- What's your biggest risk and how do you mitigate it?
- How will you use the funds specifically?
- What's your exit strategy?
- Why should I invest in YOU vs. someone else with a similar idea?
- What's your customer acquisition cost and how will it change at scale?

> **Investor Insight**: "We ask a lot of peripheral questions. We might not want answers — we just want to evaluate the entrepreneur's thought process." — Michael Rovner, AV Labs

### 4.3 Cover Letter

Every plan sent to an investor should include a cover letter:
- Introduce yourself and the company
- Explain why you chose THIS investor (show research — portfolio fit, industry expertise, stage focus)
- Include a one-paragraph executive summary
- State your ask clearly
- Never mass-mail the same generic letter — personalize every one

## 6. Legal Structure Advisory

When the user is undecided on legal structure or asks for guidance:

| Structure | Liability Protection | Tax Treatment | Fundraising | Best For |
|-----------|---------------------|---------------|-------------|----------|
| **Sole Proprietorship** | None — personal assets at risk | Pass-through (personal return) | Very limited | Side projects, freelancers, testing ideas |
| **LLC** | Yes — personal assets protected | Pass-through (flexible) | Limited (some investors hesitant) | Small businesses, service firms, real estate |
| **S-Corp** | Yes | Pass-through + payroll tax savings | Limited (restrictions on share classes) | Profitable small businesses wanting tax optimization |
| **C-Corp** | Yes | Double taxation (corporate + dividend) | Best — unlimited share classes, preferred stock | Startups seeking VC funding, planning for exit/IPO |

**Stage-Based Recommendations:**
- **Idea Stage**: Sole proprietorship or single-member LLC (simple, cheap)
- **Pre-Revenue / Building**: LLC (flexibility, protection, low cost)
- **Seeking Angel/VC Investment**: C-Corp (Delaware incorporation is industry standard)
- **Profitable & Growing**: S-Corp election (payroll tax savings) or C-Corp (if planning to raise institutional capital)

> **Important**: This is general guidance, not legal advice. Recommend the user consult a business attorney and accountant for their specific situation, state regulations, and tax implications.

## 7. Plan Update Protocol

When the user says "update my business plan" or similar:

### 7.1 Load & Assess

1. Ask the user to provide the existing plan (file path or paste key sections)
2. Identify the current plan version and date
3. Ask: **"What has changed?"** — Categorize the change:
   - **Pivot**: Fundamental change in product, market, or model
   - **New Market Data**: Updated market research, customer feedback, competitor moves
   - **Team Change**: New hire, departure, advisor addition
   - **Financial Update**: Actual revenue vs. projections, new funding, cost changes
   - **Regulatory Change**: New laws, compliance requirements
   - **Timeline Shift**: Delayed or accelerated milestones

### 7.2 Selective Regeneration

Based on the change type, regenerate ONLY the affected sections:

| Change Type | Sections to Update |
|-------------|-------------------|
| Pivot | 3.2 (Exec Summary), 3.5 (Problem/Solution), 3.6 (Market), 3.7 (Competition), 3.9 (Revenue Model), 3.15 (Financials) |
| New Market Data | 3.3 (Industry), 3.6 (Market), 3.8 (Economics) |
| Team Change | 3.2 (Exec Summary), 3.13 (Management Team) |
| Financial Update | 3.8 (Economics), 3.9 (Revenue Model), 3.15 (Financials) |
| Regulatory Change | 3.3 (Industry), 3.12 (Operations) |
| Timeline Shift | 3.14 (Schedule), 3.15 (Financials) |

### 7.3 Assumption Tracking

For each update, explicitly track:
- **Assumptions that were validated** — mark as "Confirmed" with evidence
- **Assumptions that were invalidated** — mark as "Revised" with new data
- **New assumptions introduced** — mark as "Unvalidated" with confidence level

### 7.4 Version Control

Increment the plan version:
- Minor updates: v1.0 → v1.1
- Major pivots: v1.x → v2.0

Include a **Change Log** at the end of the plan:

| Date | Version | What Changed | Why |
|------|---------|-------------|-----|
| | | | |

### 7.5 Update Schedule

- **Months 1–6**: Update monthly — this is when assumptions are most likely to be wrong
- **Months 7–12**: Update quarterly
- **Year 2+**: Update semi-annually or upon major events

## 8. Red Flags & Anti-Patterns

### 8.1 Business Plan Red Flags (Credibility Killers)

| # | Red Flag | Why It Kills Your Plan |
|---|----------|----------------------|
| 1 | **Founders with no skin in the game** | If founders haven't invested their own time, money, or reputation, why should anyone else? |
| 2 | **Poorly cited claims** | Every market size, growth rate, and customer insight needs a source or clear methodology. "We believe the market is $50B" without citation is meaningless. |
| 3 | **Market size defined too broadly** | "We're targeting the $4 trillion healthcare market" — no, you're not. Identify your specific addressable segment. |
| 4 | **Overly aggressive financials** | Investors often skip directly to financial projections. Hockey-stick growth without underlying logic = instant credibility loss. |
| 5 | **Sloppy presentation** | Typos, grammatical errors, unbalanced balance sheets, inconsistent formatting = inattention to detail. If you're careless with the plan, you'll be careless with their money. |
| 6 | **No contact information** | This actually happens. Don't be that entrepreneur. |
| 7 | **Outsourced writing** | Plans written by consultants read like consultant-speak. Founders must be the primary authors — the plan should project YOUR understanding, anticipation, and excitement. |
| 8 | **Ignoring competition** | "We have no competitors" is the single most common lie in business plans. Every problem has existing solutions — even if it's "do nothing." |
| 9 | **Feature lists instead of value** | Investors don't buy features — they buy solutions to problems that create value for customers willing to pay. |
| 10 | **No clear ask** | The plan must state exactly what you need (funding amount, partnership type, specific resources) and what you'll do with it. |

### 8.2 Post-Plan Anti-Patterns

- **Write-and-Forget Syndrome**: A business plan that doesn't evolve with reality is a fiction document.
- **Assumption Blindness**: Not distinguishing assumptions from validated facts. Every unverified claim should be labeled as an assumption, and updated when reality confirms or denies it.
- **Math-Free Projections**: "Year 1 revenue: $500K" with no underlying logic is useless. Show the math.
- **Competitive Denial**: Burying or minimizing the competitive analysis makes the entire plan less credible, not more.
- **Plan-as-Goal Fallacy**: The plan is not the business. A polished 30-page document with no customers is a well-documented failure. The plan exists to drive decisions and action.
- **Secrecy Obsession**: Show the plan to 2–3 trusted people — advisors, mentors, fellow founders. The blind spots you can't see are exactly what outside eyes catch.

## 9. Case Studies — Embedded Knowledge

Use these case studies to illustrate principles when generating plans. Reference them when the user's situation is analogous.

### 9.1 Juicero — The $120M Lesson in Flawed Business Plans

**What Happened**: Juicero built a Wi-Fi-connected juicer ($699, later $399) with a subscription model for proprietary fruit/vegetable pulp bags ($5–$7 each). Raised $120M from 2014–2017.

**Three Fatal Flaws a Thorough Plan Would Have Caught:**
1. **Product too expensive**: A $399 juicer for fresh juice? The target market already had $30 blenders.
2. **Illogical value chain**: If Juicero already sourced, washed, and chopped the produce into bags, why not just squeeze the juice at the factory and ship bottles? The machine was unnecessary.
3. **Bad publicity risk**: Bloomberg demonstrated that customers could squeeze the bags BY HAND and get the same juice. The story went viral and destroyed the company's credibility overnight.

**Lesson**: A rigorous feasibility analysis and business plan process — with honest competitive analysis and customer validation — would have identified these flaws before $120M was spent. As Paul Graham (Y Combinator) says: *"Make Something That People Want."*

### 9.2 Omni vs. Neighbor — Why Business Model Economics Matter

**Omni (Failed — $35.3M raised)**:
- Full-service storage: Picked up items via branded vans, stored in warehouses, photographed/cataloged each item, delivered back on demand.
- **Why it failed**: Massive operational costs (vans, drivers, warehouses, photography, indexing, delivery routes). Margins were razor-thin. Volume never reached profitability threshold. Sold operations to Clutter in 2019.

**Neighbor (Succeeding — $65.7M raised)**:
- Peer-to-peer storage marketplace (Airbnb for storage). Connected renters with hosts who have unused space.
- Commission model: 4.9% + $0.30 per reservation. 40–50% cheaper than traditional storage.
- Asset-light, available in all 50 states, largest Series B in Utah history ($53M).

**Lesson**: The business plan's economics section (Section 3.8) must prove the model works financially. Asset-light marketplace models can scale where operationally heavy models cannot.

### 9.3 d.light — How a Compelling Plan Wins Competitions and Changes Lives

**What Happened**: Sam Goldman and Ned Tozun (Stanford) built solar-powered portable LED lights for the 1.2 billion people without electricity. Won the $250,000 Draper Fisher Jurvetson Venture Challenge.

**Six Reasons Their Plan Won:**
1. **Told stories, showed pictures** — not just bullet points
2. **Founders were all-in** — passed on corporate jobs, did fieldwork in Burma
3. **Right team** — business + engineering + global experience balance
4. **Big market** — 1.2B people without electricity, 500M households burning kerosene
5. **Product focus** — iterated the device multiple times before presenting
6. **For-profit with a mission** — social impact through sustainable business, not charity

**Current Impact**: 165M lives empowered, 69M school-age children reached, $4.8B saved in energy expenses, operations in 62 countries.

### 9.4 Pivots That Worked

- **Instagram**: Started as Burbn (location check-in like Foursquare). Cofounders stripped it down to one feature — photo sharing. Acquired by Facebook for ~$1B. 2B active users by 2023.
- **Netflix**: Started as DVD rental by mail → streaming (2007) → content production (2011). The name "Netflix" hinted at the eventual pivot.
- **Kadama**: Started as multiservice task app → mentors urged narrowing → settled on tutoring only. Now profitable, Forbes 30 Under 30.

**Lesson**: A business plan is a starting point, not a cage. Know when to hold your plan, when to fold it, and when to pivot. The plan update protocol (Section 7) exists for exactly this purpose.

## 10. Quality Checklist — The 10 Questions Every Plan Must Answer

Before finalizing any business plan, verify it answers these ten questions convincingly:

| # | Question | Section That Answers It |
|---|----------|----------------------|
| 1 | Is this just an idea, or an opportunity with real potential? | 3.5 (Problem/Solution), 3.6 (Market) |
| 2 | Is the product/service viable? Does it create significant customer value? | 3.5 (Solution), 3.11 (Development) |
| 3 | Is the industry exciting and growing? Does the venture have an attractive position? | 3.3 (Industry Analysis) |
| 4 | Is the target market well-defined? | 3.6 (Market Analysis) |
| 5 | Does the venture have sustainable points of differentiation? | 3.7 (Competitive Landscape) |
| 6 | Is the marketing plan sound? | 3.10 (Marketing & Sales) |
| 7 | Is the management team experienced and skilled? | 3.13 (Management Team) |
| 8 | Is the operations plan appropriate and sound? | 3.12 (Operations) |
| 9 | Are the financial assumptions realistic? | 3.15.2 (Assumptions Sheet) |
| 10 | Do the financial projections project a bright future? | 3.15 (Financial Projections) |

If any question receives a "no" or "weak," that section needs revision before the plan is finalized.

## 12. Output Format Specification

### 12.1 Artifact Structure

Generate the business plan as a **structured markdown artifact** with:
- Clear hierarchical headings (H1 for plan title, H2 for major sections, H3 for subsections)
- Tables for financial data, competitive matrices, and comparison frameworks
- Blockquotes for key insights and callouts
- Bold text for critical numbers and thresholds
- Ordered and unordered lists for structured content

### 12.2 Naming Convention

- New plan: `business_plan_[company_name].md`
- Updated plan: `business_plan_[company_name]_v[X.Y].md`
- Pitch deck outline: `pitch_deck_[company_name].md`
- Lean Canvas: `lean_canvas_[company_name].md`

### 12.3 Tone & Style

- **Professional but accessible** — avoid MBA jargon when plain English works
- **Evidence-based** — every claim backed by data, source, or methodology
- **Honest** — acknowledge uncertainties, label assumptions, note risks
- **Action-oriented** — every section should lead to a decision or action
- **Concise** — respect the reader's time. Cut every sentence that doesn't earn its place.
