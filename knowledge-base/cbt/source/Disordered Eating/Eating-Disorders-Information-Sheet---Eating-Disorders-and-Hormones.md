# **Weight Suppression & Hormones** 

**How Do Eating Disorders Affect Hormones?** Eating habits and weight play an important role in our hormone levels. Disordered eating, compensatory behaviours (e.g. excessive exercise), and body weight can disrupt hormone production. Hormones that regulate metabolism, fertility, pregnancy, and bone health are particularly affected by disordered eating. 

## **Fertility in Females and Males** 

When the body is not receiving enough food, it reduces processes that require a lot of energy, including functions of the reproductive system. Low levels of the hormones oestrogen, progesterone, and testosterone can lead to reduced fertility or infertility in people experiencing an eating disorder. 



In women, symptoms may include: 

- Low libido (reduced sexual desire) 

- Absence of menstrual periods 

- Irregular menstrual periods 

In men, symptoms may include: 

- Low libido (sexual desire); 

- Loss of early morning erections;

Most people who have recovered from an eating disorder can go on to have healthy pregnancies and healthy babies. 



## **Bone Health & Osteoporosis** 

Eating disorders can have a significant impact on bone health. When the body is not receiving enough consistent nutrition, bones can lose strength and become more fragile. This can lead to osteoporosis, a condition where bones become brittle, and break more easily. 

When the body does not have adequate energy or nutrient reserves, several hormones are affected. These include sex hormones (such as oestrogen and ‑ testosterone), as well as cortisol, IGF 1 (insulin-like growth factor), and leptin. Changes in these hormones can slow bone growth and may lead to bone loss over time.

Hormonal changes associated with eating disorders are usually reversible. With consistent nourishment and a return to a body weight that is healthy for you, sexual desire and physical functioning typically improve over time. 

Although existing bone loss cannot be fully reversed, further damage can often be prevented. Restoring adequate nutrition, and supporting the body back to its natural healthy weight range are key steps in protecting bone health.

Importantly, even if you are experiencing signs of reduced fertility, it is still important to use contraception if you are sexually active and do not wish to become pregnant. Fertility can return unexpectedly, and pregnancy can occur even before any obvious signs of reproductive changes appear. 

- High levels of stress hormones (e.g., cortisol, noradrenaline) can cause sleep problems, anxiety, depression, or panic (e.g. increased heart rate) 

- Eating disorders may also impact on hormones involved in appetite, puberty and growth.
