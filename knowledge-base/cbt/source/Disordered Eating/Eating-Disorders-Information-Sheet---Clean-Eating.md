It can be hard to know what “healthy” eating really means today. This is not surprising since we are constantly being shown messages about how we “should” eat on social media, in magazines, newspapers and on TV. 

**_Mood and emotions_** : Extreme clean eating can also impact your mood. It’s not uncommon for people to feel really irritable or down while eating in this way. You may also feel guilty or anxious if you “break” your dietary rules (which is almost inevitable when the rules are very strict!). Clean eating can also be mentally and physically tiring—it takes a lot of effort and “research” to eat in this way. 

**“Clean eating”** can be described as eating only select foods from each food group (e.g., carbohydrates, proteins, fats) and cutting out foods seen as “unhealthy”, such as foods high in sugar, transfats, preservatives or additives. It also focuses on how foods are made or processed. At face value, “clean” eating may _appear_ to reflect traditional healthy eating guidelines, which advocate for flexible and nutritious eating habits (see _<u>Australian Dietary Guidelines</u>_ ). However, it is common for those attempting to eat “cleanly” to quickly progress from **_limiting_** their intake of certain food types to **_avoiding_** these foods altogether.  This strict pattern of eating is often referred to as **Orthorexia** (although it is not a formal diagnosis) and it can affect both men and women equally. 

**8 Signs Your Eating is Becoming Too Inflexible** If you’re starting to wonder if you are becoming too obsessed with eating “cleanly”, ask yourself the following questions: 

_1. Have I cut out specific foods or entire food groups?_ 

_2. Do I feel guilty after eating “bad” foods, or proud after eating good or “clean” foods?_ 

_3. Is it hard for me to eat a meal prepared by someone else?_ 

_4. Am I avoiding social events so that I can stick to my eating routine?_ 

_5. Do I judge how other people eat or encourage them to eat in a similar way to me?_ 

_6. Do I spend a lot of time planning my meals in advance?_ 

**“Clean” Eating: What are the risks?** **_Physical health_** : Extreme clean eating can pose serious risks to your wellbeing. It can impact on your physical health, including  your heart or hormonal functioning, or make you miss out on essential health, even if you are consuming enough energy each day (please refer to our handouts titled: _<u>Eating disorders: What are the risks?</u>_ and _<u>Starvation Syndrome).</u>_ 

_7. Do I choose to eat foods I don’t actually enjoy eating?_ 

_8. Do I often spend lots of time researching nutrition?_ 

_9. Is it hard for me to focus at work or school because I can’t stop thinking about food, planning my meals, or perfecting my eating?_ 

_10. Have I lost confidence in how I can eat and nourish myself?_ 

If you answered ‘yes’ to some of these questions, then it may suggest clean eating is a problem for you. 

**_Mental health_** : Extreme clean eating can also affect your mental health. We know that the more we attempt to control or limit what we eat, the more deprived we feel, which leads us to become increasingly pre-occupied with our eating choices. This can have an impact on our concentration, and interfere with our ability to work, study, or our hobbies and interests. 

**Overcoming Extreme “Clean” Eating** Here are some suggestions to help you begin to overcome problematic clean eating: 

- Follow a balanced, flexible eating plan that is based on dietary recommendations. This includes eating foods from _all_ the food groups (e.g. protein, fats, carbohydrates, fruits, vegetables and dairy) and allowing yourself (without feeling guilty!) to eat foods such as chocolate and cake in moderation. 

**_Socialising_** : Extreme clean eating can also affect our relationships. Labelling eating as “clean” implies that alternative ways of eating are “unclean” or even “dirty”.  Not only is this assumption incorrect but it can also imply a sense of righteousness and moral superiority to others who do not eat in this way.  As a result,  it may cause tension or make it difficult to share meals with family or friends, which may make you become increasingly isolated. 

- Limit the amount of time you spend researching what you “should” eat and critically evaluate the information you read (see our _<u>Evaluating Dietary Advice</u>_ handout). 

- Use the Australian nutritional standards as an objective and unbiased guide to eating healthily. 

- • Access help from a qualified professional (e.g., a dietitian, your GP, a psychologist).
